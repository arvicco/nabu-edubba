# The verb and its object

Last chapter's sentences had no verb. This chapter's have
exactly one, and the rule that governs them is the friendliest
in the language: **subject, verb, object — in that order** — and
nothing conjugates, nothing declines, nothing agrees. Word order
does all the work English endings and helpers do. Meet the
chapter's verbs of seeing and motion:

## Subject, verb, object

The pattern on characters you own outright:

## Knowing without going

A verb's object can be a place — go out a door, know the world.
The *Laozi* uses exactly that pattern to make its boldest claim
about knowledge:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">不出▢，知天下。</span><span class="translit pinyin">bù chū hù, zhī tiān xià</span><span class="gloss">"without going out the door, know the world" — the world runs on one pattern, and the pattern is visible from anywhere: insight is not mileage. The box is "door"; note the shape — verb 出 takes its object 戶 exactly as 知 takes 天下</span></div>

## The strangest entrance

And because objects are just words in the object seat, even
"nothing" can sit there:

## Where this leaves you

Two sentence-shapes now: *X, Y 也* names and defines; *subject
verb object* acts. Nearly everything you will ever read is one
of the two, dressed up. Next: how the language says "there is,"
"there is not" — and how it says no.
