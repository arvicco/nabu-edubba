# With, by, from, at

English has prepositions; Literary Chinese has small verbs that
learned the trade. 以 was "to take" and became **by means of**;
於 parks a phrase **at, in, from**; 自 is **from** — and, glued
to a verb, **oneself**; 與 is **together with**. Each hitches a
phrase to the sentence's main verb, and between them they do
most of what a case system would:

## The hitches at work

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--composed">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">王與民出。</span><span class="translit pinyin">wáng yǔ mín chū</span><span class="gloss">"the king goes out with the people" — a ruler in the fields among his farmers: the classics' favorite image of good kingship, in four characters</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">民生於土。</span><span class="translit pinyin">mín shēng yú tǔ</span><span class="gloss">"the people are born of the soil" — the farmer's plain truth: everything they are comes up out of the ground they work</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">王以民為本。</span><span class="translit pinyin">wáng yǐ mín wéi běn</span><span class="gloss">"the king takes the people as his root" — the people-as-root creed of classical statecraft: cut the root and the tree falls, whoever holds the axe</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Practice lines assembled from taught characters.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Three hitches, walked. In the first line
<span class="script">與民</span> sits between subject and verb:
<span class="script">與</span> hitches a companion — "together
with the people" — and the sentence then runs on
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/02-the-verb-and-its-object/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02's</a>
plain rails, <span class="script">出</span> with no object. In
the second, <span class="script">於土</span> hangs *after* the
verb: <span class="script">生</span> "are born," and
<span class="script">於</span> hands it the source, "from the
soil."

The third line is the one to memorize as a frame:
<span class="script">以</span> X <span class="script">為</span>
Y — "take X to be Y." <span class="script">以</span> still
remembers being the verb "take": the king *takes* the people
and *makes* them his root. The pair works like a single verb
with two mouths, and the classics reach for it constantly —
you will meet it again before this page is done.

## Look at the how

以 keeps a trace of "take" in one of Confucius' most practical
sentences — how to read a person:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">▢其所以。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shì qí suǒ yǐ</span><span class="gloss">"watch what a person does" — the first step of Confucius' three-step x-ray. The passage continues: then look at WHY he does it, then at what contents him — and no one can hide. The box is "look at"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 2.10, the first step of the three-step
    x-ray.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:002:11a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Small line, dense middle. The boxed verb "look at" takes an
object, and the object is built: <span class="script">其</span>
"his," then <span class="script">所以</span> —
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/06-the-one-who-the-thing-which/' | relative_url }}">last chapter's</a>
<span class="script">所</span> reaching into this chapter's
<span class="script">以</span>: "that *by which* (he acts)," his
means, his conduct. <span class="script">所</span> can seize a
preposition just as it seizes a verb — and this particular
fusion, <span class="script">所以</span>, went on to become one
of the commonest words in the language.

## Use is what isn't there

The *Laozi*'s wheel-hub argument, closing on 以 and 用 in one
breath — thirty spokes share a hub, and the cart works because
the hub's center is EMPTY:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--stacked">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">有之以為▢，</span><span class="translit pinyin">yǒu zhī yǐ wéi lì,</span><span class="gloss">"what is there gives the advantage,"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">無之以為用。</span><span class="translit pinyin">wú zhī yǐ wéi yòng</span><span class="gloss">"what is not there gives the use" — the clay makes the pot, but the hollow holds the water; rooms are useful because of the space inside the walls. The box in the first line is "advantage"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Laozi</em> 11, the conclusion.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR5c0057:011:1a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The couplet is one skeleton stamped twice, with a single
contrast: <span class="script">有</span> against
<span class="script">無</span>. Walk the second line:
<span class="script">無之</span> — "the absence of it," with
<span class="script">之</span> the little pronoun
(<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/06-the-one-who-the-thing-which/' | relative_url }}">chapter 06's</a>
lesson, already paying) pointing back at the hub, the pot, the
room. Then <span class="script">以為用</span> — the very frame
you just practiced, "takes that and makes the use." What is
there gives you something to hold; what is not there is the
reason it works. The grammar carries the paradox without
strain, which is the *Laozi*'s favorite trick.

## From a hair-tip; from yourself

於 marks the source in the *Laozi*'s image of patient growth,
and 自 turns knowing inward:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--stacked">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">生於▢末。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shēng yú háo mò</span><span class="gloss">"it grew from a hair-thin tip" — said of a tree two arms can barely circle: every huge thing began negligibly small. 末, the treetop-tip of Foundations, with 於 marking the source; the box is "fine-hair"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">知人者▢，自知者明。</span><span class="translit pinyin">zhī rén zhě zhì, zì zhī zhě míng</span><span class="gloss">"one who knows others is clever; one who knows himself sees clearly" — cleverness reads the room; clarity reads the reader. The box is "clever"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Laozi</em> 64 and 33.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR5c0057:064:1a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

In the first line <span class="script">生</span> is the verb
and <span class="script">於</span> hands it its source — "grew
*from* a hair-thin tip" — the same seat
<span class="script">於土</span> filled in your practice line.
The second is a matched pair of doer-phrases:
<span class="script">知人者</span>, "the one who knows
*others*" — a whole verb-plus-object folded under
<span class="script">者</span> — against
<span class="script">自知者</span>, where
<span class="script">自</span> slips in *before* the verb and
turns it inward: the self-knower. Note the seat it takes:
"know others" puts the object after the verb, "know oneself"
tucks 自 in front — reflexives break the usual order, and that
displacement is how you spot them. Then each doer gets a
one-character verdict: clever; clear-sighted.

whence, with whom, and by what means. Next: how a sentence says
