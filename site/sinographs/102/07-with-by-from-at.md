# With, by, from, at

English has prepositions; Literary Chinese has small verbs that
learned the trade. 以 was "to take" and became **by means of**;
於 parks a phrase **at, in, from**; 自 is **from** — and, glued
to a verb, **oneself**; 與 is **together with**. Each hitches a
phrase to the sentence's main verb, and between them they do
most of what a case system would:

## Look at the how

以 keeps a trace of "take" in one of Confucius' most practical
sentences — how to read a person:

## Use is what isn't there

The *Laozi*'s wheel-hub argument, closing on 以 and 用 in one
breath — thirty spokes share a hub, and the cart works because
the hub's center is EMPTY:

## From a hair-tip; from yourself

於 marks the source in the *Laozi*'s image of patient growth,
and 自 turns knowing inward:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--stacked">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">生於▢末。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shēng yú háo mò</span><span class="gloss">"it grew from a hair-thin tip" — said of a tree two arms can barely circle: every huge thing began negligibly small. 末, the treetop-tip of Foundations, with 於 marking the source; the box is "fine-hair"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">知人者▢，自知者明。</span><span class="translit pinyin">zhī rén zhě zhì, zì zhī zhě míng</span><span class="gloss">"one who knows others is clever; one who knows himself sees clearly" — 自 slipped before the verb makes it reflexive: self-knowing. The box is "clever"</span></div>
