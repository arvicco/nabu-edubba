# Can, get, want

可 said *may*. This chapter fills out the row of little verbs
that sit before other verbs: 能, *is able to*; 得, *gets (to)*;
欲, *wants to*. Beside them, two verbs of bringing-into-being —
作, to make and originate; 成, to complete and become:

## The little verbs in front

The pattern first, on your own characters:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--composed">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">民欲學而不能。</span><span class="translit pinyin">mín yù xué ér bù néng</span><span class="gloss">"the people want to learn, and cannot" — the field takes the daylight and the tax takes the rest: wanting was never the missing part</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">士欲見王，不得入。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shì yù jiàn wáng, bù dé rù</span><span class="gloss">"the officer wants to see the king — and does not get in" — the classical scene of the petitioner at the gate, credentials in hand, door shut</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Practice lines assembled from taught characters.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The modals sit exactly where English puts them: before the
verb. <span class="script">欲學</span> "wants to-learn,"
<span class="script">欲見</span> "wants to-see." Two details
are the lesson. In <span class="script">不能</span> the main
verb has vanished — "cannot (learn)": a modal may stand alone
once the verb is obvious, the same trust-the-context habit as
the dropped pronoun. And <span class="script">不得入</span>
splits the hairs of failure: not "cannot enter" (no ability?)
but "does not *get* to enter" — <span class="script">得</span>
is the modal of luck and permission, what the world lets you
do.

## What everyone wants

Confucius grants the wanting — and prices the getting:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--stacked">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">▢與▢，是人之所欲也；</span><span class="translit pinyin">fù yǔ guì, shì rén zhī suǒ yù yě;</span><span class="gloss">"wealth and rank — these are what people want;" — the boxes are "wealth" and "rank"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">不以其道得之，不▢也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">bù yǐ qí dào dé zhī, bù chǔ yě</span><span class="gloss">"but got the wrong way, I would not keep them" — no ban on wanting; only on cheating your way to it. The box is "stay-with"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 4.5, the opening.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:004:5a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The first line teaches a comfortable habit of classical style:
name the topic, then pick it back up. Wealth-and-rank stand
first (<span class="script">與</span> as plain "and" between
nouns — <a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/07-with-by-from-at/' | relative_url }}">chapter 07's</a>
give-with in its mildest life); then
<span class="script">是</span>, "these," gathers the pair up and
sits it in the topic seat of a nominal frame:
<span class="script">人之所欲也</span> — the thread, the
<span class="script">所</span>-machine on this chapter's verb
("the wanted-of-people"), the seal. In the second line the
condition rides in front as an
<span class="script">以</span>-phrase —
<span class="script">不以其道</span>, "not by their proper
way" — then <span class="script">得之</span>, get + the little
pronoun. The whole ethics of the passage hangs on that one
<span class="script">以</span>: the *how* of the getting is what
gets judged.

And the one want that cannot be cheated — asked whether two
famous refusers of a throne died bitter:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">▢仁而得仁，又何▢？</span><span class="translit pinyin">qiú rén ér dé rén, yòu hé yuàn</span><span class="gloss">"they wanted to do right, and they got to do right — what is there to resent?" — wanting to act well is already acting well. The boxes are "seek" and "resent"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 7.14.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:007:18a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Sought, and-then got: <span class="script">而</span> strings the
two verbs into a biography. Then the close,
<span class="script">又何</span>▢ — "then again, *what* would
they resent?" — and there is
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/09-the-art-of-asking/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09's</a>
law in the wild: <span class="script">何</span> is the object of
the boxed verb, and it has jumped in front of it, exactly as
question words must.

## Able to be great — by not trying

能 and 成 share the *Laozi*'s sentence on how water-like
greatness works:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">以其▢不自為大也，▢能成其大。</span><span class="translit pinyin">yǐ qí zhōng bù zì wéi dà yě, gù néng chéng qí dà</span><span class="gloss">"he never makes himself out to be great — and that is exactly why he can become great" — greatness as a side effect: chase the work, not the title. The boxes are "to the end" and "therefore," the second four chapters ahead</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Laozi</em> 34, the closing turn.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR5c0057:034:1a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The first half is one long *because*-phrase:
<span class="script">以</span> here takes a whole clause —
"by (the fact that) he to-the-end does not self-make great" —
with the topic-pausing <span class="script">也</span> you met on
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/05-the-belonging-thread/' | relative_url }}">the great Way's walking</a>
closing it off. Inside it, <span class="script">不自為大</span>
is <a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/07-with-by-from-at/' | relative_url }}">chapter 07's</a>
reflexive doing moral work: not "does not make (others)
great" — does not make *himself out* to be great. Then the
consequence: boxed "therefore," and this chapter's pair in
harness — <span class="script">能成其大</span>, "*can complete*
his greatness." Able, because unwilling: the modal row carries
the paradox.

## To make — or to pass on

作 gets the tradition's two most famous sentences about making:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--stacked">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">▢而不作。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shù ér bù zuò</span><span class="gloss">"I pass on; I do not invent" — Confucius on his own method: a transmitter of the old ways, not an author of new ones. The box is "pass on"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">萬物作▢而不▢。</span><span class="translit pinyin">wàn wù zuò yān ér bù cí</span><span class="gloss">"the ten thousand things arise — and he does not refuse them" — the wise person lets the world get on with its making instead of managing it. The boxes are a small "therein" and "refuse"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 7.1 and <em>Laozi</em> 2.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:007:1a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Watch <span class="script">作</span> change hands between the
two masters. In Confucius' mouth it is transitive making — "I
do not *author*" — set against the boxed "pass on" by a single
<span class="script">而</span> of contrast. In the *Laozi* the
same verb turns intransitive:
<span class="script">萬物作</span>▢, the ten thousand things
*arise*, doing their own making with nobody in the object seat.
One character, and the two books' whole quarrel about
creation — transmit or originate, manage or let be — fits
inside its two faces.
