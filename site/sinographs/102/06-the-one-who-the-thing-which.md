# The one who, the thing which

Literary Chinese has no word for "who" or "which" in the middle
of a sentence — and never misses them, because two small words
turn any verb into a noun. **者** makes the *doer*: 知者, "the
one who knows." **所** makes the *done-to*: 所見, "that which is
seen." Between them they manufacture every "-er" and every
"what-X-does" the language needs:

## The wise and the humane

The most loved 者-sentence in the *Analects* — two doers, two
landscapes:

  <div class="reading-lines">

## Why they called him Cultured

謂 in its home key — asked why a minister earned the grand
posthumous name 文, Confucius answers with the naming verb
itself:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">是▢謂之文也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shì yǐ wèi zhī wén yě</span><span class="gloss">"that is why they call him 'the Cultured'" — he was quick, he loved learning, and he was not ashamed to ask people beneath him. The box is a small "because-of" — next chapter's word; 謂之 X, "call it X," is the naming pattern itself</span></div>

## The books quote themselves

書 and 詩 are common nouns — writing, song — and also proper
names: *the Documents* and *the Odes*, two of the classics
every classical author knew by heart. When a text cites the
songbook, it says 詩云, "the Odes say" — and you can now read a
real citation whole:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">詩云：之子于▢。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shī yún: zhī zǐ yú guī</span><span class="gloss">"the Odes say: this young woman goes to her wedding" — a scholar quoting the Peach Tree song to make a point about households; the box is "marry". 云 opens quotations from BOOKS the way 曰 opens them from speakers</span></div>
  </div>

## Where this leaves you

者 and 所 turn verbs into nouns; 謂 names things; 詩云 quotes
the tradition. Next: the little words that hitch a phrase to a
verb — with, by, from, at.
