# The one who, the thing which

Literary Chinese has no word for "who" or "which" in the middle
of a sentence — and never misses them, because two small words
turn any verb into a noun. **者** makes the *doer*: 知者, "the
one who knows." **所** makes the *done-to*: 所見, "that which is
seen." Between them they manufacture every "-er" and every
"what-X-does" the language needs:

## Doer and done-to

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--composed">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">知者知天，行者行道。</span><span class="translit pinyin">zhī zhě zhī tiān, xíng zhě xíng dào</span><span class="gloss">"the knower knows heaven; the walker walks the Way" — a doer is named by his doing, and goes on doing it: you are what you repeatedly do</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">王之所見，非民之心也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">wáng zhī suǒ jiàn, fēi mín zhī xīn yě</span><span class="gloss">"what the king sees is not the people's heart" — the oldest problem of palaces: reports climb the stairs, truth stays in the street</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">人之所謂文，古之道也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">rén zhī suǒ wèi wén, gǔ zhī dào yě</span><span class="gloss">"what people call culture is the way of the ancients" — the classics' own creed about themselves, said with 所謂, "the so-called"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Practice lines assembled from taught characters.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Walk them. In the first line <span class="script">知者</span> is
verb + <span class="script">者</span> — "the knowing one" — and
the minted noun immediately sits in a subject seat and does its
own verb: knower knows, walker walks. The chiasmus is the
lesson: <span class="script">者</span> turns doing into a doer,
and the doer back into doing.

The second line stacks three chapters. <span class="script">所見</span>
is <span class="script">所</span> + verb — "the seen," the
done-to; <span class="script">王之</span> hangs
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/05-the-belonging-thread/' | relative_url }}">last chapter's</a>
thread on it: "what the king sees." Then
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/03-having-and-lacking/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a>
<span class="script">非</span> denies the identity, and
<span class="script">也</span> seals: *what-the-king-sees* ≠
*the-people's-heart* — a whole political science in the bones of
a nominal sentence.

And the third: <span class="script">所謂</span> is the pair
fused — "the so-called" — one of the most-used phrases in the
language, alive to this day. What follows it is whatever the
world names; what follows the comma is what the thing really
is, in <a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/01-the-sentence-without-a-verb/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a>
frame.

## The wise and the humane

The most loved 者-sentence in the *Analects* — two doers, two
landscapes:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">知者▢水，仁者▢山。</span><span class="translit pinyin">zhī zhě yào shuǐ, rén zhě yào shān</span><span class="gloss">"the wise delight in water; the humane delight in mountains" — the quick mind loves what moves, the steady heart what stands. The boxes are "delight in", twice the same character</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 6.23.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:006:30a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The skeleton, twice: <span class="script">知者</span> "the wise
one" in the subject seat — the doer-noun this chapter mints —
then the boxed verb "delights in," then the object:
<span class="script">水</span>, water. The answering clause
swaps every filling and keeps every seat:
<span class="script">仁者</span> for the doer, mountain for
water. Twin skeletons with the seats refilled is this
language's favorite way to think
(<a href="{{ '/sinographs/102/02-the-verb-and-its-object/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02</a>
called it parallelism-as-argument) — and here the argument is a
portrait: the quick mind loves what moves, the steady heart
what stands.

## Why they called him Cultured

謂 in its home key — asked why a minister earned the grand
posthumous name 文, Confucius answers with the naming verb
itself:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">是▢謂之文也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shì yǐ wèi zhī wén yě</span><span class="gloss">"that is why they call him 'the Cultured'" — he was quick, he loved learning, and he was not ashamed to ask people beneath him: the name was earned, and this line hands down the verdict</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 5.15, on Kong the Cultured.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:005:17a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Two small machines meet here. <span class="script">是</span>▢ is
"this" plus the boxed "because-of" — next chapter's word — "for
this reason." Then <span class="script">謂之文</span>: the
naming verb, and right after it a <span class="script">之</span>
you have not met before — not the thread but a little pronoun,
"him." A <span class="script">之</span> directly after a verb is
that verb's object, pointing back at whoever was just
mentioned: "call *him* Cultured." One character, two lives —
thread between nouns, pronoun after verbs — and, as always,
position tells you which one you are looking at.

## The books quote themselves

書 and 詩 are common nouns — writing, song — and also proper
names: *the Documents* and *the Odes*, two of the classics
every classical author knew by heart. When a text cites the
songbook, it says 詩云, "the Odes say" — and you can now read a
real citation whole:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">詩云：之子于▢。</span><span class="translit pinyin">shī yún: zhī zǐ yú guī</span><span class="gloss">"the Odes say: this young woman goes to her wedding" — a scholar quoting the Peach Tree song to make a point about households; the box is "marry"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    A Han-era ritual text citing the <em>Odes</em>.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1g0009:012:17b</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

<span class="script">詩云</span> is the machine to hold on to:
name of book, then <span class="script">云</span> — the
quotation-opener for texts, as <span class="script">曰</span> is
for speakers. Everything after the colon is the songbook's own
voice. Inside the quote, one more surprise from the busiest
character in the language: <span class="script">之子</span> is
"THIS young woman" — in the Odes' old dialect
<span class="script">之</span> could still point, a third life
the classical prose you are learning keeps only in frozen
quotations like this one. File it as a curio, not a tool.

## Where this leaves you

者 and 所 turn verbs into nouns; 謂 names things; 詩云 quotes
the tradition. Next: the little words that hitch a phrase to a
verb — with, by, from, at.
