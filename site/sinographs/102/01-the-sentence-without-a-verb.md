# The sentence without a verb

Literary Chinese can say *X is Y* without any word for "is." Set
the two things side by side, close with 也 — the "indeed" you
have carried since Foundations — and the sentence is complete:
**X，Y 也**. Grammars call it the *nominal sentence*, and it is
the classical language's favorite way to define, to classify,
and to judge. This chapter gives you the pattern and the cast of
persons the classics most love to define:

## X, Y 也

The pattern first, in sentences where every character is yours:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--composed">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">王者，天之子也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">wáng zhě, tiān zhī zǐ yě</span><span class="gloss">"the king is the son of heaven" — no verb anywhere: king set beside heaven's-son, sealed with 也. ## Humaneness is being human

The *Records of Ritual* defines Confucianism's central virtue in
four characters, on exactly this frame:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">仁者人也。</span><span class="translit pinyin">rén zhě rén yě</span><span class="gloss">"humaneness is being human" — the definition is a pun and a thesis at once: 仁 (humaneness) and 人 (human) share one sound, and the book's claim is that being good to others IS what being a person means. Every character is yours</span></div>

## When a noun turns verb

Push the pattern one step and a bare noun can carry a whole
claim. Asked how government works, Confucius answers with four
nouns, each doubled into its own little sentence:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">君君，臣臣，▢▢，子子。</span><a class="say" href="{{ '/assets/audio/lines/102-01-jun-jun-chen-chen.mp3' | relative_url }}" title="hear it"><span class="translit pinyin">jūn jūn, chén chén, fù fù, zǐ zǐ</span></a><span class="gloss">"let the lord be a lord, the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son" — the whole theory of order in four doubled words: things run when each person actually IS what their role demands, starting at the top. The second word of each pair does the predicate's work — a noun serving as its own verb. The boxed pair is "father"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 12.11.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:012:21a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

## The noble word

君 doubles into 君子 — "the lord's son" by etymology, "the noble
person" by two and a half millennia of usage: the person whose
conduct, not birth, earns the title. You met the phrase in
Foundations' final reading; here is its home sentence:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">人不知而不▢，不亦君子乎？</span><span class="translit pinyin">rén bù zhī ér bù yùn, bù yì jūn zǐ hū</span><span class="gloss">"to go unrecognized and not resent it — is that not a noble person?" — the third line of the Analects' opening: your worth does not depend on an audience. The box is "resent"</span></div>
  </div>

## Where this leaves you

One sentence-shape, and it is everywhere: definitions (仁者人也),
titles (天子), politics (君君臣臣). Next chapter the verb
arrives — and with it the sentence that *does* something.
