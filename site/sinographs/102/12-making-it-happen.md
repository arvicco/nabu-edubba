# Making it happen

To make someone do something, Literary Chinese needs no "make …
do" scaffolding: **使 X V** — *send X to V, cause X to V* — one
word does it all. Its natural theater is statecraft, so the
chapter hires the state:

千 quietly repairs the number ladder — you have had one through
ten, a hundred, and the myriad, but never the thousand between.

## The causative, paid in full

The little clause an earlier chapter had to box now reads
whole — and shows the pattern:

## The whole saying

And with 千 and 國 in hand, the full sentence 使民以時 closes —
Confucius on running a real country, end to end:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">道千▢之國，▢事而▢，▢用而▢人，使民以時。</span><span class="translit pinyin">dào qiān shèng zhī guó, jìng shì ér xìn, jié yòng ér ài rén, shǐ mín yǐ shí</span><span class="gloss">"to lead a state of a thousand chariots: take the work seriously and keep your word; spend little and care for people; and call the people to labor only in the right season" — a whole theory of government in fifteen characters: diligence, honesty, thrift, care, timing. The boxes are "chariot", "respectfully", "keep-faith", "sparingly", "care-for"</span></div>

## Held in common, knowable for ages

公 pays the oldest box in this course, and 世 answers a question
about the far future:

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--stacked">
  <div class="reading-lines">

## Where this leaves you

The causative runs the state, the state runs on seasons, and
the sentence that owed you three chapters of boxes has paid
every one. Next: likeness — good as water, and each other.
