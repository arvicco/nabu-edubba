# The old and the written

Every text you have read so far talks about itself — about what
is old, why things follow, and what is written. This chapter
teaches it those words:

古 is the chapter's quiet triumph of components: ten and mouth,
both from your very first weeks, joined into "what ten
generations of mouths have carried." 故 adds a striking hand and
turns the old into a *because*. 文 — the patterned body — grew
from tattoo to writing to everything writing carries; 天文 is
the sky's pattern, the stars. And capitalized, as it were, 書
and 詩 are two of the classics themselves: the *Documents* and
the *Odes* — the 詩書 the tradition never stops citing.

## Where this leaves you

Ninety-two characters. The course can now say *old* and
*therefore*, and name the two books it has been quoting all
along. Next: the people who did the quoting — lords and
ministers, officers and the common folk, and the little word
that asks the tradition's favorite question: *what?*
