# The busiest words

Open any page of any classic and count: the commonest character in
the whole classical corpus is not a king or a mountain. It is a
little grammar word. Here are six characters from the very top of
the table:

之 is **rank one**: nothing in three thousand years of classical
writing is written more often. 不 is rank two, 也 rank four, 有
rank nine. Learn these six and the statistics of every page tilt
toward you at once.

## The fifth move: borrow the whole word

<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/04-roots-tips-borrowed-sounds/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 04</a>
showed characters borrowing a *sound-half* (the 木 inside 沐). The little words go further: how do you draw *of*?
*not*? *indeed*? You cannot — so the script **borrowed whole
pictures for their sound alone** and let the original meanings
fall away. The foot-over-a-line was some concrete old word; the
grammar word *zhī* simply moved into the empty house.[^jiajie]
That is why the table's "how to see it" column suddenly hedges:
with borrowed words, the picture-story is history, not meaning.
The stroke shapes are still yours to write — 之 in three strokes,
也 in three — but from here on, frequency is the teacher, and the
picture is only the address.

## The first reading with no boxes at all

You have met this clause twice — first as a wall of boxes, then
half-lit. Today it opens completely:

Look at what each word does: 未 "not yet" (<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/04-roots-tips-borrowed-sounds/' | relative_url }}">chapter 04</a>), 之 "it"
standing for the thing in question, 有 "there has been," 也
closing the statement like a struck bell. This is classical
grammar in miniature — negation, object, verb, final particle —
and every stroke of it is yours.

## Have, and the composing numbers

有 goes straight to work on a line you know:

十 multiplies the counting you already own, because classical
numbers compose: <span class="script">二十</span>, *èr shí*, is
"two tens" — twenty; <span class="script">三十</span>, *sān shí*,
thirty. And in old dates the annals write "fifteen" as *ten and
five* — with, of all things, 有 standing in for its cousin 又
"again": the two hands of this chapter meeting inside a number.

And with 之 the two-character phrases of chapters past grow into
real classical syntax, read straight: <span class="script">天下之大</span>,
*tiān xià zhī dà*, "the greatness of all under heaven";
<span class="script">山中之人</span>, *shān zhōng zhī rén*, "a
person amid the mountains." The little 之 hinges them — X 之 Y,
"the Y of X" — and you will now see that hinge on every page you
ever read.

## Where this goes

Thirty-five characters, and the reading percentages have jumped:
these six alone are about one character in every sixteen of the
whole classical corpus. Next: an arrow, the verb "to know," and
the beard that got borrowed — the sentence about the unrecognized
gentleman is one chapter away from opening.

[^jiajie]: The loan move is the tradition's *jiǎjiè*, fourth of
    the six scripts; the foot-reading of 之's early form follows
    the standard account in Qiu Xigui, *Chinese Writing* (2000).
