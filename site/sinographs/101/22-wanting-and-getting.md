# Wanting and getting

Doing was last chapter's business. This one is about why anyone
does anything — wanting, getting, giving — and about what
everyone then says:

得 is gain drawn as luck — a cowrie picked up on the road. 與
gives with four hands and settles down as the little "with"
between nouns. 欲 wants with an open mouth. 皆 says *everyone*,
謂 says *what people call a thing*, and 然 — a roast borrowed
for "so" — will close this chapter's most famous sentence.

## What everyone wants

Confucius grants the wanting — and prices the getting:

## What everyone says

皆 makes its entrance in the *Laozi*'s opening move — the moment
everyone agrees on what is beautiful, something has gone wrong:

## We did it ourselves

The best ruler, says the *Laozi*, is barely noticed. Under him
the work gets finished — last chapter's words — and the people
all say — this chapter's words — one thing:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">▢成事▢，而百▢皆謂我自然。</span><span class="translit pinyin">gōng chéng shì suì, ér bǎi xìng jiē wèi wǒ zì rán</span><span class="gloss">"the work is done, the task complete — and the people all say: we did it ourselves" — the highest praise of leadership: to lead so lightly that no one feels led. 自然, "so of itself", is the Laozi's word for what happens naturally — you will meet it wherever Chinese is written. The boxes are "achievement", "follow through", and "families" — 百-families, "the hundred families," is classical for *everyone*</span></div>
  </div>

## Where this leaves you

A hundred and twenty-seven characters, and with them the whole
economy of motive: want it, get it, give it, and mind what
everyone will say. Next: the state those people live in — a
thousand chariots, the wide earth, and the wind of a lord's
example.
