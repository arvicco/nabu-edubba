Look hard at the third one. 明 is not a new picture — it is 日 and
月, which you now own, standing together. You have just watched the
system's central move happen: **characters are built from
characters.**

## Pictures first

The oldest layer of the script is drawings: 人 a person, 日 the
sun, 月 the moon — and a mountain, a gate, an eye, all waiting in
the chapters ahead. If the whole
system worked like that, it would be a picture-book, and reading it
would be guessing. It is not. Drawable things ran out almost
immediately — how do you draw *bright*? *yesterday*? *trust*? The
script's history is the history of answers to that question.

## The four moves

The tradition sorts characters into six kinds; four of them are the
ones that matter for a reader, and you now hold examples of
three:[^liushu]

1. **Draw it.** 人, 日, 月 — a pictograph, for things with shapes.
2. **Point at it.** Add a mark to a drawing where the meaning
   sits — the line over the person's head in 天. A small class,
   coming properly in the next chapters with the numbers.
3. **Combine meanings.** 明, sun-and-moon, *bright* — two
   characters lending their senses to a third. Memorable, but
   still limited: most ideas do not decompose into two pictures.
4. **Borrow a sound.** The move that built the real bulk of the
   stock — write a hard word with an easy word that *sounds the
   same*, then add a meaning-hint so readers know which of the
   sound-alikes is meant. Here is one you can already half-read:

<p class="script sign-strip" aria-label="The character time, split into sun and a sound-carrier">時&ensp;=&ensp;日&ensp;+&ensp;寺</p>

時 is *shí*, "time, season." Its left half is your 日 — the sun,
carrying the **meaning** (time is sun-business). Its right half, 寺
*sì*, is there only to carry the **sound**. Nine of every ten
characters in the dictionary are built exactly like this: a meaning
half and a sound half.[^phono] The picture-story you may have heard —
"every character is a little painting" — is charming and wrong, and
you will read better the moment you drop it: most characters are a
*hint at the sense* plus a *hint at the sound*.

at all. Boxes are characters still ahead of you:

Notice what reading with boxes teaches even before the boxes fill:
classical lines run in parallel clauses of equal length, and once
you can anchor two or three characters, the architecture of the
whole sentence shows through.

## Where this goes

You now know the four ways a character can mean, and you own six
characters across three of them. The next chapter turns to the
strokes themselves — how a character is actually written, and why
stroke order is information, not etiquette. The sound-borrowing
engine of move four becomes the main road soon after: it is how
this school will take you from dozens of characters to hundreds
without hundreds of new pictures.

[^liushu]: The six-kind classification (*liùshū*) comes from the
    *Shuowen jiezi* postface (2nd c. CE); the four presented here
    are the kinds a modern reader actually meets, following the
    standard modern account in Qiu Xigui's *Chinese Writing*.

[^phono]: The nine-in-ten figure for phono-semantic compounds is
    the standard estimate for the mature character stock; see Qiu
    Xigui, *Chinese Writing* (2000), ch. 1.
