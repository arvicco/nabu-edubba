# Lords and the people

The readings keep talking about people who rule and people who
are ruled. This chapter puts names to the whole cast — and adds
the little word that questions all of them:

君 commands, 臣 looks up, 民 carries the load, 士 stands between
them with an axe — or a writing brush — and 公 is both the
feudal title and everything held in common. In the *Analects*
the 君子, the "lord's son", becomes the book's hero: noble by
conduct, not birth. And 何 —
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/10-the-sentences-tools/' | relative_url }}">chapter 10's</a> 可 wearing a person —
asks the question the whole tradition turns on: *by what right?*

## Let the lord be a lord

Duke Jing of Qi asks about government; the Master answers with
the most famous eight characters of political philosophy — four
words, each said twice:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">

## Who weighs most

Mencius, asked what a state rests on, gives the scandalous
ranking — first and last clause readable at one box each; the
middle clause, "the altars of soil and grain come second," waits
in the caption:

## Where this leaves you

Ninety-eight characters, and the whole classical stage is cast:
lord and duke, officer and minister, and the people they answer
to. Next the stage gets its directions — out and in, the
quarters of the world, and being *at* a place at all.
