A crowd of mouths, one tree with three marks — then the move that

品 is the combining move from last chapter, played as a tripling:
three of your 口 make *many kinds* — the world sorted. The next
three are the pointing move, played on a taught part: mark the
bottom of 木 and you have named the root; mark the top, the tip;
let the top sprout young growth and you have *not yet*.[^wei]
Classical argument runs on the marked pair — 本末, root and
branch-tip, the essential versus the incidental — and runs on 未
constantly, because "not yet" is how the classics say *still
un-*.

## The fourth move

Now look hard at the last two rows, because they are the engine of
the entire script. 沐 means *to wash the hair*. Its left half is
your 水 — squeezed into three quick strokes to fit the side, the
one shape-change this chapter teaches. Water is the **meaning**
half: washing is water-business. But the tree on the right has
nothing to do with washing. 木 is there because it *says mù* — and
so does 沐. The tree lends its sound and falls silent.

味 plays the same trick with your newest character: mouth for the
meaning — flavor is mouth-business — and 未, *wèi*, for nothing
but the sound of *wèi*, flavor. A character you learned as "not
yet" turns out to moonlight as a pure syllable.

## Where this stretch ends

Twenty-nine characters, and all four of the script's moves —
drawing, pointing, combining, sound-borrowing — each learned on
characters whose every part you own. From here the course rides
the fourth move: most of what remains is a meaning-hint plus a
sound you already know, which is how a few hundred characters will
open into thousands. The road is real text all the way — the
sayings, the Songs, the Changes — and the boxes get rarer every
chapter.

[^wei]: The young-growth reading of 未 is the received account
    (*Shuowen*); its classical workload as the negative "not yet"
    is borrowed sound, senses per Kroll.

[^phono]: The nine-in-ten figure for phono-semantic compounds in
    the mature character stock is the standard estimate; see Qiu
    Xigui, *Chinese Writing* (2000), ch. 1.
