中, 上, 下 are the numbers' cousins — nothing in the world looks
like *middle* or *above*, so the character simply points. And
before you ask: the number pattern you learned in <a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a> does
not continue either. Four is <span class="script">四</span>, a
borrowed character, not four strokes — the script tried stacking
strokes, felt the cliff coming, and switched moves. Keep that
switch in mind; it is the whole history of the system in
miniature.

Watch 曰 next to 日 the sun — the pair are the script's most
famous look-alikes. 曰 sits wider than tall, and its inner stroke
stops short of the right side; the sun is tall, and its inner
stroke runs wall to wall. The books will make you tell them apart
on every page, so start now. As for 王: the account in the table
is the one the tradition loved — the king as the vertical joining
heaven, earth, and humanity — but the earliest forms look more
like an axe blade, the plain emblem of power. Both stories are
told; the character is older than its explanations.[^wang]

## What a stroke is

A character is not drawn, it is **written**: a fixed sequence of
strokes, each made in one movement, each with a direction. The
inventory is small — a horizontal drawn left to right, a vertical
drawn top to bottom, falling strokes to either side, a dot, a few
hooks and turns. Every character you will ever learn, however
dense, is a stack of these, written in an order as fixed as the
spelling of an English word.

Stroke order is not etiquette. It is how the script stays legible
when written fast — a character written in the fixed order
degrades into a predictable running shape, which is why a
practiced reader can read handwriting that shares almost no
outline with the printed form. It is also how the tradition
*counts*: dictionaries have filed characters by stroke count for
nearly two thousand years, and "how many strokes?" is still the
first question asked of an unknown character. Three rules cover
most of what you will ever write: top before bottom, left before
right, a horizontal before the vertical that crosses it.

## The master says

子 began as a drawing of a baby and never lost that sense — but in
the books you will read it is above all a **title**: *the Master*.
曰 is the tradition's opening quotation mark with a voice:
"said:". Put them together and you have
<span class="script">子曰</span>, *zǐ yuē*, "the Master said" — the
two-character hinge on which the entire *Analects* swings. You can
now read one of its most famous sayings almost whole:

The pointing characters earn their seats the same day. 日中,
"sun-middle," is noon; and the first line of the *Spring and
Autumn Annals* — the chronicle that gave its name to an age —
dates the world by the king:

And with 天 from <a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a> you can already read two words the
classics never tire of: <span class="script">天下</span>, *tiān
xià*, "all under heaven" — the world; and
<span class="script">天王</span>, *tiān wáng*, "the king by
Heaven" — the Son of Heaven, as the chronicle calls him on page
after page.

## Where this goes

Eighteen characters, every one simple — drawings and pointed
marks, nothing built from anything. That was deliberate, and the
wait is over: next chapter, characters start combining, and every
part of every compound will already be yours.

[^wang]: For 王 as axe blade in the earliest script, see Qiu
    Xigui, *Chinese Writing* (2000); the heaven-earth-humankind
    reading is the *Shuowen*'s, beloved of the tradition.
