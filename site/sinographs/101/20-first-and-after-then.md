# First and after, then

One character outranks every other character you have not met:
則, "then" — twenty-third most frequent in the whole classical
corpus, and the hinge of its arguments. It looks hard. It is
two easy pictures standing together, so this chapter buys the
pictures first:

貝 is rare on the page and priceless in the workshop: cowrie
shells were money before coins, and the shell went on marking
value inside bigger characters long after the shells themselves
went out of circulation. 刀 you could still cut with. Put 刀
under <a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/15-the-ten-thousand-things/' | relative_url }}">chapter 15's</a>
八 — which began life meaning "apart" before the number eight
borrowed it — and you have 分, dividing. Put the knife, slimmed
to 刂, beside the cowrie, and you have 則: the ancients cut
their standards into bronze — the received graph keeps a cowrie
where the oldest bronzes show a ritual cauldron — and a standard
cut into metal is a **rule**. From "rule" the classical language
took its favorite hinge: *X 則 Y* — where X holds, Y follows,
as the rule dictates. That one hinge carries half the reasoning
you will ever read.

## First things first — then

The *Great Learning* has been yours since
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/14-here-and-now/' | relative_url }}">chapter 14</a>;
its opening page walks a ladder — root and tip, end and
beginning — and then says why the ladder matters. With 先, 後
and 則, its next line reads almost whole:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">知所先後，則▢道矣。</span><span class="translit pinyin">zhī suǒ xiān hòu, zé jìn dào yǐ</span><span class="gloss">"know what comes first and what comes after — then you are near the way" — the book's whole method in eight characters: most of wisdom is getting the ORDER right, what to fix first and what can wait. The box is "near"; 所…, "that which…", does its usual work</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">

Watch the hinge do its work: *know the order, **then** the way
is near.* From here on, whenever a classical sentence sets a
condition, 則 will be standing at the turn.

## Where this leaves you

A hundred and sixteen characters. The cowrie and the knife were
bought cheap and spent well — 分 and 則 came from parts you
could already name. Next: the words for doing, making, and
being able — and the *Laozi*'s most famous way of doing nothing
at all.
