# The sentence's tools

The little words gave the sentence its joints. Now it goes to
work — starting with the third most frequent character in the
whole classical language, the biggest one you have not yet met:

以 is a workhorse: 以水 "with water", 以言 "by words". Doubled
with 可 it makes the classical "can": 可以, "can thereby". 自
faces two ways — *self* before a verb (自知, "know oneself") and
*from* before a place. 丁 is here as 可's right-hand piece and
earns its keep at once in the table above. And 用 is what you do
with a tool — which makes it the right character to close the
row.

## Knowing others, knowing yourself

The *Laozi* line every self-help book has borrowed since — and
your 明, the sun-and-moon brightness from
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/03-the-master-says/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03</a>,
is the payoff word:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">知人者▢，自知者明。</span><span class="translit pinyin">zhī rén zhě zhì, zì zhī zhě míng</span><span class="gloss">"the one who knows others is clever; the one who knows himself is enlightened" — the Laozi's ranking: reading other people is a skill, reading yourself is wisdom, and the second is the rarer and higher; one box for "clever"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Laozi</em> 33.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR5c0057:033:1a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Watch 自 do its work: 知人 is "knowing OTHERS"; 自知 is "knowing
YOURSELF" — the object folds back into the knower. That fold is
all 自 needs to say.

## The usefulness of nothing

The most famous praise of empty space ever written — a wheel's
hub, a pot's hollow, a room's doorway — ends in a sentence built
almost entirely of your characters:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">有之以為▢，無之以為用。</span><span class="translit pinyin">yǒu zhī yǐ wéi lì, wú zhī yǐ wéi yòng</span><span class="gloss">"what is there gives the advantage; what is NOT there gives the use" — the pot's clay is the having, the pot's hollow is the using; one box for "advantage"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Laozi</em> 11 — the chapter of the wheel, the pot, and
    the room.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR5c0057:011:1a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

以為 — "take as, thereby make" — appears twice and carries the
whole argument. 有 and 無, the having and the not-having,
stand at the heads of the clauses; 用 lands
the point. This is what "the sentence at work" means: every
grammatical muscle in that line is one you already own.

## Can, thereby

可以 is how the classics say that something *suffices* — and the
most teacherly sentence in the *Analects* ends on it:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">可以為▢▢。</span><span class="translit pinyin">kě yǐ wéi shī yǐ</span><span class="gloss">"…can thereby be a teacher" — the close of the saying "warm up the old and know the new, and you can be someone's teacher"; the boxes are "teacher" and a final particle whose story waits three chapters</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 2.11.
    <code>urn:nabu:kanripo:KR1h0004:002:12a</code> · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The saying's first half — "warm up the old and know the new"
(wēn gù ér zhī xīn) — is this course's own method: every reading
here reheats old characters around each new one. When the boxed teacher-word
arrives in a later chapter, the sentence will close for you
whole.
