These are the second of last chapter's four moves — **point at
it** — in their purest form. Nothing in the world looks like
*two*; the character simply shows you twoness. And before you ask:
no, the pattern does not continue. Four is
<span class="script">四</span>, a borrowed character, not four
strokes — the script tried stacking strokes, felt the cliff coming,
and switched moves. That switch is the whole history of the system
in miniature.

## What a stroke is

A character is not drawn, it is **written**: a fixed sequence of
strokes, each made in one movement, each with a direction. The
inventory is small — a horizontal drawn left to right, a vertical
drawn top to bottom, falling strokes to either side, a dot, a few
hooks and turns. Every character you will ever learn, however
dense, is a stack of these — 一 has one stroke, 三 has three, and
the densest characters of the classical books have over twenty,
written in an order as fixed as the spelling of an English word.

## Why order is information

Stroke order is not etiquette. It is how the script stays legible
when written fast — a character written in the fixed order
degrades into a predictable running shape, which is why a
practiced reader can read handwriting that shares almost no
outline with the printed form. It is also how the tradition
*counts*: dictionaries have filed characters by stroke count for
nearly two thousand years, and "how many strokes?" is still the
first question one asks of an unknown character. Write 三 top
stroke first, middle second, bottom last; write every character
top-to-bottom, left-to-right, horizontals before the verticals
that cross them. Those three rules cover most of what you will
ever write.

Nine characters taught, and you are reading number-phrases —
三人 "three people", 一日 "one day", 二三 "two or three, a few" —
that the classical books use on nearly every page.
