為 and 無 are the loan move at its grandest. A hand leading an
elephant — the very picture of getting work done — was borrowed
for the plainest verb in the language: *do, make, act as*. A
dancer with streaming sleeves was borrowed for *there is not*;
the dance eventually took itself a new character and left this one
holding pure absence.[^wu] And 我, the classical "I," began as a
kind of halberd — three thousand years of writers calling
themselves by an old weapon without ever thinking of it.

## The triple paradox opens

<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/04-roots-tips-borrowed-sounds/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 04</a>
closed on three characters of the *Laozi*'s advice.
With 為 and 無 the whole saying unfolds:

無為, *wú wéi* — "doing-without-doing" — is the most famous two
characters of Daoist thought, and you now read both. But what
does doing-without-doing *mean*? Not laziness. The *Laozi*'s
claim is that forcing things breaks them: the best ruler barely
seems to govern, and the state runs; the best work does not feel
like straining; push against the river and it drowns you, move
with it and it carries you. 為無為 is advice to act the way
water acts — effective without effort, never forcing. Now notice
the pattern of the sentence: VERB 無 VERB, three times;
classical prose loves a mold, and once you see the mold you can
pour any verb into it.

## Where this goes

Forty-five characters, 18 percent of the corpus. Next: the chain
止 → 正 → 是 — stop, upright, this — and the evening that names
things, ending on a saying you will read without a single box.

[^wu]: The elephant-and-hand reading of 為 and the dancer reading
    of 無 are the standard paleographic accounts; see Qiu Xigui,
    *Chinese Writing* (2000). The dance later re-armed itself as a
    separate character; classical texts leave 無 to absence.
