# The order of strokes

Six characters in one breath — the easiest numbers ever written,
and three marks that point:

All six are one move — **point at it**. Nothing in the world looks
like *two* or *middle* or *above*, so the character simply shows
you the fact: two strokes for twoness, a mark over the line for
aboveness, a pole through the center for the middle. And before
you ask: no, the number pattern does not continue. Four is
<span class="script">四</span>, a borrowed character, not four
strokes — the script tried stacking strokes, felt the cliff
coming, and switched moves. Keep that switch in mind; it is the
whole history of the system in miniature.

## What a stroke is

A character is not drawn, it is **written**: a fixed sequence of
strokes, each made in one movement, each with a direction. The
inventory is small — a horizontal drawn left to right, a vertical
drawn top to bottom, falling strokes to either side, a dot, a few
hooks and turns. Every character you will ever learn, however
dense, is a stack of these, written in an order as fixed as the
spelling of an English word. Write 三 top stroke first, middle
second, bottom last; write 中 box first, then plant the pole.

Stroke order is not etiquette. It is how the script stays legible
when written fast — a character written in the fixed order
degrades into a predictable running shape, which is why a
practiced reader can read handwriting that shares almost no
outline with the printed form. It is also how the tradition
*counts*: dictionaries have filed characters by stroke count for
nearly two thousand years, and "how many strokes?" is still the
first question asked of an unknown character. Three rules cover
most of what you will ever write: top before bottom, left before
right, a horizontal before the vertical that crosses it.

## Read the numbers count the world in

where everything comes from, with your whole number batch on

With 天 from <a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a>, 上 and 下 hand you two words the classics
never tire of: <span class="script">天下</span>, *tiān xià*, "all
under heaven" — the world; and <span class="script">天上</span>,
*tiān shàng*, "up in heaven." Eleven characters, and you are
already reading the words empires argued about.
