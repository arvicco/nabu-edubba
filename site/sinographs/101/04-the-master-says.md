# The master says

Three characters of people and speech — including the two that
open more classical sentences than any others:

子 began as a drawing of a baby and never lost that sense — but
in the books you will read, it is above all a **title**: *the
Master*. 曰 is the tradition's opening quotation mark with a
voice: "said:". Watch it next to 日 the sun — the pair are the
script's most famous look-alikes. 曰 sits wider than tall, and its
inner stroke stops short of the right side; the sun is tall, and
its inner stroke runs wall to wall. The books will make you tell
them apart on every page, so start now. Put them together and you have
<span class="script">子曰</span>, *zǐ yuē*, "the Master said" —
the two-character hinge on which the entire *Analects* swings.
As for 王: the account in the table is the one the tradition
loved — the king as the vertical that joins heaven, earth, and
humanity — but the earliest forms look more like an axe blade,
the plain emblem of power. Both stories are told; the honest
answer is that the character is older than its explanations.[^wang]

This is what progress feels like in this school: the same real
text, reread with more of it yours. Every 子曰 you will ever meet —
and the *Analects* alone has hundreds — is now two characters of
solid ground at the start of the sentence.

## The king's first month

王 earns its place the same day it is taught. Here is the first
line of the *Spring and Autumn Annals* — the chronicle of
Confucius's home state, the book that gave its name to an entire
era of history. Its famous opening dates the world by the king:

And with 天 from
<a href="{{ '/sinographs/101/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a>
you can already read the annals' name
for the highest ruler of all: <span class="script">天王</span>,
*tiān wáng*, "the king by Heaven" — the Son of Heaven, as the
chronicle calls him on page after page.

## Fifteen characters in

Count what you hold: 人 大 天 · 日 月 明 · 一 二 三 · 山 水 中 ·
子 曰 王 — fifteen characters, three or four percent of
*everything written* in the classical corpus, and already enough
to read number-phrases, landscapes, dates, and the master's own
opening formula. The next stretch of the course adds the small
grammar-words that stitch these into whole sentences — the
highest-frequency characters of all — and the readings start
shedding their boxes fast.

[^wang]: For 王 as axe blade in the earliest script, see Qiu
    Xigui, *Chinese Writing* (2000); the heaven-earth-humankind
    reading is the *Shuowen*'s, beloved of the tradition and
    repeated by Dong Zhongshu.
