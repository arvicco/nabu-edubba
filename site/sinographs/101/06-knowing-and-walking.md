矢 is pinned far ahead of its own frequency for one reason: 知 is
built on it, and this course never shows you a compound before you
own its parts. 而 is the chapter's borrowed word — a beard, of all
things, lent to the busiest clause-joiner in the language (rank
five of the whole corpus). And 言 puts your 口 to work as a
meaning-part: the mouth with sound rising out of it, the seed of
the entire talking family of characters.

## The unrecognized gentleman

The first page of the *Analects* holds three sayings; here is the
third, one box from open:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">人不知而不▢。</span><span class="translit pinyin">rén bù zhī ér bù yùn</span><span class="gloss">"to go unrecognized — and not resent it" — person · not · know · and-then · not · (resent): five of six characters yours, and the saying continues "…is that not a gentleman?"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Analects</em> 1.1.

Watch 而 work: it joins "others do not know him" to "he does not
resent it" with a single stroke of grammar — *and-yet*. Half of
classical prose is clauses hinged on exactly this character.

And 言 goes to work at once in the word the tradition uses for
its own proverbs: <span class="script">有言</span>, *yǒu yán* —
"there is a saying." When a classical author writes 有言, a
quotation is coming; you now read the signpost. The character
itself waits one more chapter for its starring line — the most
famous sentence ever written about getting names right.
