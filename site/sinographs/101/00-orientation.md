# Orientation

You are at the start of the foundations course of the sinograph
school. It has no prerequisites. In Edubba every chapter begins by
handing you signs, so here are your first three characters, right

Write each one a few times before reading on — two strokes for the
person, three for big, four for heaven. They are not a random
starter set: 人 is the seventh most frequent character in the whole
classical written tradition, and the three of them are a single
picture growing.

Most characters are built from earlier characters like this — that
is the single most useful fact about the whole system, and this
course teaches it from the first page.[^chain]

## The promise

This school does something the shelf of modern textbooks does not.
The characters have been writing Chinese for more than three
thousand years, and for most of that time what they wrote was the
classical written language — the language of the sayings, histories,
laws, and poems that every literate person in East Asia once read.
This course teaches the characters **on that road**: by its end you
will read real classical lines in the original, and every character
you meet will have earned its place by how often the old books
actually use it.

You have, in fact, already started. Here is a real line — one of
the most famous sentences in the tradition, from the *Laozi*.
Characters you have not learned yet are boxed ▢; watch what you can
already read:

Three characters in, and a sentence from twenty-four centuries ago
is already coming apart into pieces you own. That is the whole
method of this school: signs first, real text immediately, nothing
in a reading you have not been taught — the boxes shrink chapter by
chapter.

## What a character is

One character is one syllable of the spoken language, and — in the
classical language — usually one whole word. 人 is the word
"person," said *rén*. There is no alphabet underneath: the writing
does not spell the sound out of smaller sound-letters. Instead,
characters are built out of **other characters**, the way 天 is
built on 大 is built on 人 — sometimes for meaning, more often (as
you will see in the next chapters) for sound. A pictograph like 人
is where the system starts, but pictures are a small minority of
the character stock; the system's real engine is combination.

## The voice you will read in

Nobody alive knows exactly how the classical language sounded when
these lines were written, and this course will not pretend
otherwise. What it gives you is what the field itself uses when it
reads the classics aloud: the modern Mandarin readings, written in
pinyin with their tone marks — <span class="translit pinyin">rén,
dà, tiān</span>. Think of it as the standard modern voice for an
ancient text, the way Latin is read today in a modern accent. Later
courses add other voices the same characters carry — the
Sino-Japanese readings of kanji among them — and the honest history
of the sounds gets its own chapter down the road.

## How this course runs

- **Every chapter opens with one to three new characters** — chosen
  by real frequency in the classical books, adjusted only where a
  simpler or more picturable character makes the road smoother.
- **Every character gets one keyword** — *person*, *big*,
  *heaven* — its own and nobody else's, your permanent handle on it.
- **Every chapter reads real text**, cited to its source, with
  untaught characters honestly boxed ▢ — never invented classroom
  sentences.
- **Components before compounds.** You will never be asked to
  memorize a character as an unanalyzed picture when it is really
  two things you already know stacked together.

## Where this sits in the school

This is the foundations course: how the writing system works, on a
first real inventory of characters. The literacy track that follows
it — Sinographs 102 — teaches the classical language itself,
grammar and all, on the same principle. Deeper courses go on to the
script's own history, back through bronze and oracle-bone forms,
and outward to the characters' lives in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.
You need none of that yet. You need 人, 大, 天 — and you have them.

[^chain]: The person→big→heaven chain follows the traditional
    analysis going back to the *Shuowen jiezi*; modern paleography
    reads 天 from the earliest forms as a figure with a marked
    head. See Qiu Xigui, *Chinese Writing* (2000), the standard
    account of how the character system is built, and Kroll,
    *A Student's Dictionary of Classical and Medieval Chinese*
    (2015), this course's default authority for classical senses.
