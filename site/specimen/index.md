# Specimen: three signs and a royal seal

<p class="chapter-standfirst">This is a <strong>layout specimen</strong>,
not a real course chapter: it exercises every element a course chapter
uses — exposition, a sign table, native script beside transliteration,
and a graded reading of a real text with its citation. The cuneiform
school's actual chapters will replace it. Everything shown about the
signs and the text, however, is real.</p>

## A first idea: signs that are not read aloud

Cuneiform writes Sumerian with a mix of word-signs and syllable-signs —
and with a third kind of sign that a reader sees but never pronounces.
A **determinative** is a sign placed before or after a word to say what
kind of thing it names. The sign 𒀭 (*an*, "heaven"), written before a
name, marks it as a god's name; the sign 𒆠 (*ki*, "earth, place"),
written after a name, marks it as a place. In transliteration these
are shown raised or in braces: *{d}* and *{ki}*.

## Reading: the seal of a governor of Umma

Officials under the kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur rolled their
seals onto everyday clay documents. The seal below appears on a
delivery record of ox-hides from Umma. Its eight short lines are a
formula you will learn to read at sight: the king's full titulary,
then the seal-owner presenting himself as the king's servant. Note
𒀭 *{d}* before the royal name and 𒆠 *{ki}* after the city names —
and *lugal* 𒈗, twice.
