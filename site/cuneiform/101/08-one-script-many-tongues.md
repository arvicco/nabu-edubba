# One script, many tongues

Every earlier chapter has treated cuneiform as one thing: a set of
signs, a way of pressing them into clay, a system for turning marks
into meaning. That is true, and it is also only half the story. The
other half is time and geography. The same writing system that
started as an accountant's shorthand went on to serve a dozen
languages across roughly three thousand years, from the marshes of
southern Iraq to the coast of Syria and the plateau of Iran. This
chapter sketches that arc and that spread — coarsely, since the fine
detail belongs to later, specialized courses. Think of it as a map of
where this school's later offerings, the [2xx courses]({{ '/cuneiform/' | relative_url }}),
will eventually take you.

## The arc, in broad periods

Cuneiform's story runs in phases that most handbooks agree on, even
where exact dates are debated. What follows is the standard outline,
kept deliberately coarse.

**Late fourth millennium BCE — origins.** The earliest antecedents of
the script appear around the city of Uruk, in the far south of
Mesopotamia, as a tool of administration: counting goods, recording
rations, tracking labor. This is proto-cuneiform, pictographic and
not yet tied to any one spoken language.

**Third millennium BCE — Sumerian.** Over the following centuries the
script grows from an accounting aid into a full writing system,
capable of recording connected language — and the language it first
records at length is Sumerian. Royal inscriptions, letters,
literature, and enormous administrative archives survive from cities
across southern Mesopotamia; the seals and bricks you read in
chapter 6 belong to this world.

**Later third millennium onward — Akkadian joins in.** Speakers of
Akkadian, a Semitic language wholly unrelated to Sumerian, adopt the
same script for their own tongue. This shift is already visible under
the Sargonic kings and becomes thoroughly established over the
centuries that follow. From this point on, cuneiform is no longer a
Sumerian-only tool — it is a writing system available to more than
one language, which is exactly the theme of this chapter.

**Old Babylonian period — the classical age.** By the time of
Hammurabi, Akkadian in its Babylonian form is a mature literary and
legal language written in cuneiform, and this period supplies much of
what later ages, and later scholars, treat as the classical standard
— including the scribal schools you met in chapter 7. Sumerian, by
now no longer anyone's native language, persists as a learned and
liturgical one, copied and studied the way Latin would be studied
long after Rome.

**Late Bronze Age — an international script.** By the middle of the
second millennium BCE, Akkadian cuneiform has become something close
to a diplomatic lingua franca across the whole eastern Mediterranean
and Near East. The clearest evidence is startling if you have not met
it before: the Amarna letters, a cache of correspondence between the
Egyptian royal court and its neighbors and vassals, are written not in
Egyptian hieroglyphs but in Akkadian cuneiform — the shared language
of international affairs, even for a kingdom with its own venerable
writing system.

**First millennium BCE — Assyrian and Babylonian libraries.** Cuneiform
continues as the working script of both Assyria and Babylonia,
reaching one of its best-known high points in the library assembled
at Nineveh under the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal — tens of thousands of
tablets of literature, omens, medicine, and administration, much of it
deliberately collected and copied.

**Decline.** Across the later first millennium BCE, Aramaic — written
in an alphabet, on perishable materials, quicker to learn and write —
spreads as the everyday language of the region, and Greek follows
after Alexander. Cuneiform does not vanish overnight; it retreats
into specialist hands: temple archives and astronomical scholarship,
kept by a shrinking community of scribes who increasingly treat the
old script as learned and antiquarian rather than everyday.

**First century CE — the last tablets.** The latest securely dated
cuneiform documents are astronomical texts, the work of scholars
still maintaining the tradition's technical core long after its
everyday use had ended elsewhere. After that, as far as the surviving
record shows, the tradition falls silent — until its rediscovery and
decipherment in the modern era, the subject of the next chapter.

## One script, borrowed again and again

The remarkable thing about this arc is not just its length but its
breadth: cuneiform did not stay bound to the language it began with.
Sumerian is a language isolate — it belongs to no known family — and
grammatically agglutinative, building words by stringing strings of
fixed elements together. Akkadian, by contrast, is Semitic, related to
Hebrew and Arabic, with a grammar built on consonantal roots and
internal vowel patterns. These are, by any measure, very different
languages. Yet Akkadian scribes wrote their language in essentially
the same script Sumerian scribes had used — keeping many Sumerian
word-signs as logograms (a Sumerian sign standing for an Akkadian
word, read aloud in Akkadian) while writing the grammar and the rest
of the vocabulary syllabically, sound by sound, in Akkadian's own
phonology. This is exactly why chapter 3's discussion of polyvalency
matters as more than a curiosity: a single sign carrying several
possible readings is precisely what let one inventory of signs serve
two unrelated languages at once, and it is also why so many signs in
this school's inventory carry a Sumerian reading and an Akkadian
reading side by side.

Once that pattern was set, other languages followed it. Elamite, the
language of southwestern Iran, was written in a cuneiform script
adapted from Mesopotamian models for its own sounds and grammar.
Hittite — an Indo-European language, a distant relative of English
and Latin rather than of Sumerian or Akkadian — was recorded by
scribes in Anatolia in a cuneiform likewise borrowed and reshaped.
Hurrian and, later, Urartian, languages of the northern and eastern
fringes of the cuneiform world, took the same borrowed system and
trimmed it further to their own needs. In each case the borrowing
language kept much of the machinery you have already learned — signs
with logographic and syllabic values, determinatives marking
categories of word — while pruning and re-tuning the sign inventory
to fit sounds and grammar the script was never originally designed
for.

Two further cases push past straightforward borrowing. At the coastal
city of Ugarit, on the Mediterranean, scribes devised a script made
of wedge-shaped strokes pressed into clay — cuneiform in appearance
and technique — that was in fact a true alphabet, a couple dozen
signs each standing for one consonant, unrelated in structure to the
logographic-syllabic system used everywhere else: it looks and is
impressed like cuneiform, but works on entirely different principles.
And in the first millennium BCE, the Achaemenid Persian court
commissioned what amounts to a largely new invention: Old Persian
cuneiform, a small, mostly alphabetic-syllabic set of signs created
for royal inscriptions, wedge-shaped in form but owing little of its
sign inventory to the older tradition it visually imitates.

## Forms that changed, too

Everything above concerns which languages used the script; the signs
themselves were not static either. Across three thousand years, sign
shapes moved from the more pictorial, loosely arranged forms of the
earliest periods toward the increasingly abstract, rotated, and
standardized wedge patterns of the classical and later periods —
tighter, more regular, more removed from any pictorial origin. The
general direction is well established in the standard handbooks; the
detailed, period-by-period paleography — learning to date a tablet by
its sign forms alone — is a skill this school defers to a dedicated
3xx course, since it takes real practice with many exemplars to
acquire.

## Where each tongue lives in this school

If this chapter has done its job, it has turned "cuneiform" from a
single mental box into a family tree. This foundations course teaches
the writing system itself; the languages that ran on it belong to
other courses in the [cuneiform school]({{ '/cuneiform/' | relative_url }}).
Sumerian and Akkadian each get a dedicated literacy track — courses
102 and 103. Elamite, Hittite, Hurrian and Urartian, Ugaritic, and Old
Persian each have a planned course of their own in the 2xx range, one
per adapted tradition. The first-millennium library world and the
paleography that traces sign forms across the centuries belong to the
3xx special topics. None of those courses exist yet in full, but the
map is now in front of you; the account above follows the outline
given in standard references on the script, including Finkel and
Taylor's *Cuneiform*.
