# How signs mean

This is the spine of the course. Every reading you will ever do —
in this course, in the literacy tracks, in a museum — runs on the
machinery of this chapter. It is genuinely one idea deep, but the
idea has four gears, and the gears mesh. Read it twice.

## Gear one: signs for words

The oldest and simplest arrangement: one sign, one word. The sign
<span class="script">𒆳</span> writes the Sumerian word *kur*,
"mountain" — and you can still see the three peaks in it. The sign
<span class="script">𒀭</span>, an eight-pointed star, writes *an*,
"heaven". Signs used this way are called **logograms** (word-signs).

A writing system of pure logograms can inventory sheep and record
rations — which is roughly what the earliest tablets do. What it
cannot easily do is write a *name*, a grammatical ending, or the word
"because". For that, writing needed a trick.

## Gear two: the rebus leap

The trick is the **rebus**: borrow a sign not for what it *depicts*
but for how its word *sounds*. The sign
<span class="script">𒀀</span> depicts water and writes the word *a*,
"water". But Sumerian had many other uses for the sound /a/ — in
names, in grammar. So scribes wrote those too with
<span class="script">𒀀</span>: the picture of water, sounding as
plain */a/*, meaning nothing watery at all.

This is the single most important invention in the history of
writing. The moment signs could carry *sound*, writing stopped being
an inventory system and became able, in principle, to record anything
a mouth can say. Every writing system you know pulls this same lever.

## Gear three: signs for syllables

Once the rebus leap is made, a stock of signs settles into regular
duty as **syllabograms** — signs read as syllables (*a*, *mu*, *ba*,
*na* …) regardless of their pictorial past. Mature cuneiform mixes
the two gears freely in one line: logograms for the big content
words, syllabograms for names, particles, and grammar. Akkadian
scribes later pushed this even further, keeping Sumerian logograms as
learned shorthand inside Akkadian sentences — but that story belongs
to chapter 09.

## Gear four: silent classifiers

Some signs are written but never read aloud. A **determinative** is a
sign placed before or after a word to tell the reader what *kind* of
thing it names. Before a god's name, scribes wrote
<span class="script">𒀭</span> — the *an* sign in a third role, as
the classifier of divine names. After a city or land, they wrote
<span class="script">𒆠</span> (*ki*, "earth, place"). A reader sees
the classifier and knows what species of word is coming; a listener
never hears it. In transliteration these are printed in braces:
*{d}* and *{ki}*.

## The price: one sign, many values

The gears come at a cost that defines cuneiform for every learner.

**Polyvalency** — one sign, several readings. You have already met
the worst offender: <span class="script">𒀭</span> is the word *an*
("heaven"), the word *diŋir* ("god"), and the silent determinative
*{d}*. The sign <span class="script">𒌓</span> is *ud* ("day") and
*utu* ("sun"). Which value applies? Context — and after some
practice, context answers instantly, exactly as English readers
handle "read" (present) and "read" (past) without noticing.

**Homophony** — several signs, one sound. Sumerian had more than one
sign pronounced *e*, more than one *u*. Modern scholars keep them
apart with index numbers: *e₂* (in plain ASCII, *e2*) names the
particular *e*-sign that writes "house" — the sign
<span class="script">𒂍</span> in this site's own name. The numbers
are a modern filing system, not something ancient; a scribe just knew
which sign wrote which word.

While we are at it, the rest of the modern notation you will meet in
every reading here, in the field-standard style used by the CDLI and
Oracc corpora: sign values in lowercase with indices (*e2*, *u4*),
determinatives in braces (*{d}*, *{ki}*), and hyphens joining the
signs that spell one word. None of it exists on the clay; all of it
is scaffolding for us.

## How a reader actually copes

Four habits do the work, and this course trains all of them:

1. **Formulas.** Enormous amounts of cuneiform are formulaic — royal
   titles, date lines, receipts. Learn one formula and you read a
   thousand documents.
2. **Determinatives as road signs.** A *{d}* or a *{ki}* instantly
   maps the shape of the phrase around it.
3. **Frequency.** A modest number of signs does most of the work —
   the reason this course's inventory is chosen by computed frequency
   and graphic simplicity rather than by tradition (chapter 11
   documents both criteria, sign by sign).
4. **Writing.** A sign your hand knows, your eye resolves faster.
   Which is exactly where the next chapter takes you.
