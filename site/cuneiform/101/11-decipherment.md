# Decipherment

Everything in this course — every sign, every reading, every claim
about how the system works — passes through you because a small
number of people, working mostly without knowing whether they would
succeed, worked out how to read it again. Cuneiform was not a script
handed down through unbroken tradition, the way the Latin alphabet
was. It went out of use, entirely, and for well over a thousand years
no living person could read a line of it. This chapter tells that
recovery in outline, the way the standard histories of decipherment
tell it — coarse on dates, careful on the logic that actually
cracked the problem.

But first, two signs. They are not decoration: they are the kind of
sign that made decipherment possible.

Why open a history chapter with two syllable signs? Because signs
like these — signs that carry pure sound — are what let cuneiform
spell <em>names</em>. You met that in <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/07-of-gods-and-men/' | relative_url }}">chapter 07</a> (your own name can
only be spelled, not word-signed), and names, as you are about to
see, were the decipherers' crowbar: the one thing a lost script and
a known history could hold in common. A sign like 𒈾 keeps a spelled
name honest, syllable by syllable.

## A script nobody could read

By the early centuries of the Common Era, cuneiform writing had
stopped. The languages it had served — Sumerian long dead as a
spoken tongue, Akkadian fading after it — were gone from use, and the
script went with them. The stone-carved and clay-pressed wedges that
remained, at ruined mounds across Iraq and Iran, were visible to
anyone who traveled there. Nobody could read them. They were not
even reliably recognized as writing; travelers described the marks
on the ruins of Persepolis as ornamental, or as some kind of
patterning, without a clear sense that they encoded language at all.

Recovering the script therefore had to start from zero: not
translating a known language written in an unfamiliar alphabet, but
working out that these wedge-shaped marks were writing in the first
place, and only then asking what they said.

## Careful copies before careful reading

Progress began with better copies. Earlier European travelers had
brought back sketches of the Persepolis inscriptions, but the signs
were too imprecisely rendered to analyze. The turning point,
as the standard histories tell it, was the German traveler and
surveyor Carsten Niebuhr, whose late-eighteenth-century copies of the
Persepolis monuments were accurate enough to work from. Niebuhr made
an observation that later proved essential: the same monuments carried
not one cuneiform script but three distinct ones, carved side by
side. Whatever these inscriptions said, they said it in three
different systems at once — a strong hint, in hindsight, that the
same content had been rendered for three different audiences or
languages.

That observation split an impossible problem into three smaller
ones. One of the three scripts had, comparatively, very few distinct
sign-shapes, and appeared to mark word boundaries — features that
made it look far more approachable than the other two. It became the
door decipherers went through first.

## The first wedge: Old Persian

That simplest of the three scripts recorded Old Persian, and its
decipherment is credited, in the standard telling, largely to Georg
Friedrich Grotefend, working around the turn of the nineteenth
century. Grotefend reasoned from context rather than from any prior
key: royal inscriptions of this general kind, in languages already
known, tended to follow a set formula naming a king, his royal title
(a phrase along the lines of king of kings), and his father's name.
By matching repeated groups of signs in the Persepolis texts against
that expected pattern, and against known Persian royal names from
Greek and other historical sources, he proposed readings for a
handful of signs and for royal names such as that of Darius. It was
a narrow beachhead — a proposal about a few signs and
a few words, not a working knowledge of the language — but it held
up, and later scholars, above all Henry Rawlinson, extended it into a
far fuller reading of Old Persian cuneiform over the following
decades.

## Behistun

The single find the standard histories treat as decisive is the
Behistun inscription: a monumental relief and text carved by order of
the Persian king Darius I high on a cliff face in western Iran, far
above the plain and difficult to reach. Behistun mattered because it
repeated one long inscription in three languages — Old Persian,
Elamite, and Babylonian — carved together, exactly the multilingual
pattern Niebuhr had already noticed at Persepolis, but longer and
richer than anything found there.

A trilingual text of real length, in a script already partly broken
for one of its three languages, was the closest thing cuneiform
studies had to a Rosetta Stone. Rawlinson copied it — by the standard
accounts, at real physical risk, working from a narrow ledge on the
cliff face over more than one expedition in the 1830s and 1840s — and
that copy, combined with the Old Persian reading already achieved,
gave scholars a long, reliable bridge into the other two languages of
the inscription, Babylonian above all.

## Babylonian gives way

Old Persian used comparatively few signs and worked in an alphabet-like
way. Babylonian cuneiform was a different order of difficulty
entirely: hundreds of signs, most of them syllabic rather than
alphabetic, and many signs readable with more than one sound value or
more than one meaning depending on context — polyvalence, in the
field's term. Cracking it took the combined work of several scholars
across the 1840s and 1850s, working partly in competition and partly
building on one another's results: Rawlinson, extending the Behistun
bridge from Persian into Babylonian; Edward Hincks, an Irish
clergyman whose close analysis of sign patterns established, against
real resistance, that Babylonian signs were syllabic and routinely
polyvalent — a structural insight that unlocked far more of the
script than any single word-reading could; and Jules Oppert, who
brought comparative rigor to the language once behind the signs.
Between them, the case that cuneiform could genuinely be read, and
not merely guessed at, became strong enough to state as a claim worth
testing.

## The test of 1857

That test came in 1857, and the standard histories present it as
decipherment's decisive public moment. The Royal Asiatic Society in
London had a newly discovered inscription — a prism text of the
Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser I — sent independently to four scholars
working on Babylonian: Rawlinson, Hincks, Oppert, and William Fox
Talbot. Each translated it separately, under sealed conditions,
without comparing notes. When the four translations were opened and
compared, they agreed with one another closely enough, in substance,
to settle the question: this was not four people independently
inventing compatible fictions. Cuneiform, and the Akkadian language
written in it, could be read.

## A second language nobody expected

One more discovery followed, and it surprised the field more than the
others. Beneath the Babylonian material, texts kept surfacing that
were written in the same cuneiform signs but did not read as
Akkadian at all — a different language entirely, older, with its own
grammar. Some scholars doubted at first that this second language was
real, suspecting it might be some scribal code or cipher layered onto
Babylonian rather than an independent tongue. The doubt did not hold:
ancient bilingual word-lists and literary texts, pairing this
language line by line with Akkadian translations, let scholars
reconstruct it as what it was — Sumerian, the language the script had
originally been built to write, surviving Babylonian and Assyrian
texts. Cuneiform, it turned out, had not served one ancient language
behind its wedges. It had served at least two, layered across three
thousand years, and Sumerian had been sitting underneath Babylonian
the entire time, waiting to be told apart from it.

## What decipherment did not finish

It is worth closing honestly, because that is the standard the field
holds itself to and the standard this course tries to meet. The
work begun by Niebuhr, Grotefend, Rawlinson, Hincks, Oppert, and
their contemporaries did not finish cuneiform. Three things remain
open today.

The earliest phase of the script, the proto-cuneiform of the fourth
millennium BCE touched on in earlier chapters, still resists reading
as language in any full sense — its earliest tablets can often be
counted and categorized, but not read the way a sentence is read.
Sumerian grammar and phonology, despite long study built on those
bilingual lists, still carry real points of scholarly disagreement —
how certain forms worked, how the language actually sounded, are not
closed questions. And the practical arithmetic is
stark: hundreds of thousands of cuneiform tablets survive, museum
collections still hold large numbers never transliterated, and the
world's working cuneiformists number only in the hundreds. New texts
surface faster than they can be read.

That last gap is not a footnote — it is a large part of why a school
like this one exists. Reading ability is the scarce resource in this
field, more scarce than the tablets themselves. Every reader this
course trains, however modestly, is a small answer to a real and
current shortage, not just an exercise in history.
