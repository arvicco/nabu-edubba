# The tablet house

Cuneiform did not teach itself. Every scribe who ever pressed a
stylus into clay had first sat in a schoolroom, copying signs a
teacher had copied before them, back through a chain of apprenticeship
that ran for three thousand years. The Sumerians called that
schoolroom the **é-dub-ba-a** — conventionally translated "tablet
house," the scribal school. It is where this course's title comes from, and it is where
this chapter goes: not to another sign or another text type, but to
the institution that produced every sign and text you have met so
far.

## What we know, and how we know it

Most of what can be said with any confidence about the é-dub-ba-a
comes from a specific place and time: the Old Babylonian period,
roughly the early second millennium BCE, and above all the city of
Nippur, where excavators found houses whose back rooms were full of
student exercise tablets. Strikingly, some of the best evidence is not
administrative record-keeping about schools but *literature about
school* — a small body of Sumerian compositions, evidently written
for or by the schools themselves, that describe the scribal
curriculum, complain about teachers, and dramatize a bad day in the
classroom. These texts survive precisely because they were popular
school exercises: students copied them over and over as part of their
training, so dozens of copies come down to us where a single
administrative memo might not survive at all. The composition modern
scholars know best is usually given the nickname "Schooldays," known
chiefly from the editions and translations of Samuel Noah Kramer, who
did much of the pioneering work of reassembling it from tablet
fragments scattered across museum collections.

## A story about a bad day at school

"Schooldays" is worth summarizing, not quoting, because its charm is
in the shape of the story rather than any one line. A schoolboy
narrates his day: he wakes, hurries to school for fear of being late,
and is late anyway. Once there, the trouble compounds — his teachers
and monitors find fault with his work, his posture, his talking out
of turn, his coming and going, and each fault earns him a caning. It
is a small comic catalogue of institutional discipline, the kind of
complaint any student in any century might recognize. The boy goes
home and, in the version of the story scholars have reconstructed,
persuades his father to invite the teacher to dinner. The father
feeds the teacher well, gives him gifts and a place of honor, and the
mollified teacher praises the boy's work and his family, reversing
the day's earlier verdict. Whether read as gentle satire, as a real
window onto classroom life, or as both at once, it is the closest
thing we have to a first-person account of what the é-dub-ba-a felt
like from the inside — and its very existence tells us schools were
common enough, and schoolboys resentful enough, to make a joke like
this land.

## How the training worked

Stripped of the schoolboy's complaints, the standard accounts of Old
Babylonian scribal education describe a curriculum that moved from
the mechanical to the literary. Students began with the signs
themselves: copying sign lists, syllable lists, and word lists
(what scholars call the lexical-list tradition) until the basic
repertoire of cuneiform was second nature. From there the curriculum
moved to short, formulaic texts with practical value — proverbs,
model contracts, model letters — texts that taught scribal formulas
alongside penmanship. Only later did students copy the great works of
Sumerian literature: myths, hymns, laments, royal praise poetry, the
kind of texts that make up the literary canon later generations
inherited. The whole apparatus was, in effect, training for a
profession: a scribe leaving the é-dub-ba-a was expected to draft a
contract, a letter, or an administrative record competently, and the
literary curriculum layered prestige and cultural literacy on top of
that practical core.

Two roles recur in the school texts and in modern descriptions of the
curriculum. The **ummia** was the senior teacher, the master of the
house of tablets, addressed with real deference in the literature (and
mockery, when a schoolboy's patience ran out). Alongside the ummia
stood an older student, sometimes rendered in translation as the
"big brother," who by the standard accounts helped supervise younger
students' work, corrected exercises, and stood as a kind of
apprentice-instructor between master and beginner — a structure that
let a small teaching staff manage a graded curriculum.

The exercise tablets themselves survive in enormous numbers, and a
recurring physical format tells its own story about method. Many
student exercises are small, rounded, lentil-shaped tablets: the
teacher would write a model — a sign, a line, a short passage — on
one face, and the student would copy it on the other, sometimes
several times over, sometimes with the student's attempt visibly less
assured than the model beside it. Other exercises are larger,
multi-column tablets carrying long stretches of a lexical list or a
literary text, copied out in full. Between the two formats, the
day-to-day discipline of learning to write reveals itself with unusual
directness: model and copy, side by side, on the same lump of clay.

## Who the scribes were

Scribal training produced professionals for every domain that needed
writing: administrators tracking grain and labor, officials drafting
and witnessing contracts, letter-writers serving households and
palaces, and temple personnel maintaining the ritual and literary
texts of the cult. Literacy of this kind was a specialist skill, not
a general one — most people in these societies could not read or
write, and scribal training was the path into a genuine profession,
one that could lead to positions of real administrative responsibility.
The great majority of attested scribes were men, and the school
literature itself reads as a boys'-school story. But the record is
not uniformly male: scholarship on the Old Babylonian period,
particularly at sites such as Sippar, has identified women who held
the title of scribe, some associated with temple communities of
cloistered priestesses who needed their own literate administrators.
The evidence is real but comparatively sparse next to the mass of
male scribal careers the tablets document, and it is best kept general
rather than pinned to specific numbers or names not firmly established
in the standard literature.

## The first dictionaries

The lexical lists that opened a student's education were not simply
drill — they were reference works, and in a real sense they are the
ancestors of dictionaries and encyclopedias. Lists of signs, of
professions, of trees, of stones, of geographical names, were compiled,
copied, and recopied for centuries, occasionally expanded or adapted
but recognizably continuous across enormous stretches of time. A list
copied by a student in one century could descend, with real fidelity,
from a list compiled a thousand years earlier. That same
lexical-list tradition — organized signs, arranged systematically,
carried forward by copying — is the direct ancestor of the reference
this course itself builds toward: chapter 10 gathers every sign this
course has taught into exactly that kind of list, in the same spirit,
if a much shorter one, as the scribes who compiled theirs on clay.

## Colophons, pride, and libraries

Scribes signed their work. A **colophon** — a closing note appended
after a text was copied — could record the scribe's name, sometimes a
teacher's or owner's name, sometimes the number of lines the tablet
contained, sometimes a note about the condition of the source it was
copied from. None of this was required by the text itself; it is
visible evidence of scribal pride, a signature appended to a job
completed and checked. Multiply that pride and that copying discipline
across centuries, and libraries become possible: purposeful
collections of tablets, assembled and maintained because the texts on
them were considered worth preserving. The most famous example, and
the one this course will return to with real detail, is the library
of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh — a royal collection
assembled in the seventh century BCE that preserved enormous
quantities of the literary and scholarly tradition the é-dub-ba-a had
been copying, in one form or another, for two thousand years by then.
The fuller story of that library belongs to chapter 08, and of
decipherment to chapter 09; here it stands as the summit that all the
small, unglamorous discipline of sign lists and cane strokes was, in
the end, building toward.

## Why this site is called Edubba

The name of this site is not decoration. Edubba borrows the
Sumerians' own word for their scribal school because the shape of the
work is the same: signs copied until they are second nature, lists
worked through in order, model and exercise laid side by side, moving
from the mechanical toward the literary. You began this course with
signs and syllables, the way an é-dub-ba-a student began; by chapter
06 you were reading real inscriptions, the way a trained scribe read
real contracts and letters. You are, in every way that matters, in
the same apprenticeship the Old Babylonian schoolboy complained about
in verse — minus, this course promises, the caning.
