# From tokens to signs

Writing was not invented to record poetry, prayer, or kings. On the
evidence of the earliest tablets themselves, it was invented to stop
people arguing about sheep. This chapter follows the trail from
counters of clay to true signs — and shows the two transformations
that turned recognizable little pictures into the abstract wedge
clusters you will be writing from chapter 04 on.

## Counting before writing

For thousands of years before the first tablet, people across the
Near East used small clay **tokens** — cones, spheres, disks — in
shapes that seem to have stood for counted goods. In the widely
cited reconstruction developed by Denise Schmandt-Besserat, these
tokens were the seed of writing: sealed into clay envelopes to
guarantee a tally, then impressed on the envelope's surface so the
contents could be read without breaking it — at which point the
impressions, not the tokens, were doing the work. The details of
this token theory are debated among specialists, as the standard
introductions note, but the broad setting is not: the road to
writing runs through accounting, and its first monuments are
receipts.

## Uruk: the first tablets

The earliest things we can properly call written tablets appear in
the late fourth millennium BCE in the city of Uruk in southern
Mesopotamia — the same world of temples and administrators you met
in chapter 01. The oldest are almost purely numerical; then come
tablets that pair numbers with small drawn pictures: a head of
grain, a jar, a fish, an ox head. Scholars call this stage
**proto-cuneiform**. It is bookkeeping of real sophistication — and
it is not yet the full writing of language: word order, grammar, and
names are mostly beyond it, and much of it still cannot be *read* as
sentences today, only counted and classified.

The leap out of that limitation is the rebus principle — using a
picture for its sound — which you will meet properly in the next
chapter. Here we stay with the *shapes*, because between the first
pictures and classical cuneiform stand two great mechanical
transformations.

## Transformation one: the quarter turn

Compare early and later tablets, and a famous oddity appears: the
signs *rotated*. Signs that begin as upright pictures lie, in the
mature script, on their backs — turned roughly ninety degrees
counterclockwise. The standard handbooks connect this with changes
in how scribes held and ruled tablets as formats grew; whatever the
full cause, the effect on the signs was profound. A picture on its
back is already halfway to being an abstract mark: once the head no
longer stands upright, it stops asking to be seen as a head.

## Transformation two: the wedge rebuild

Chapter 01 gave you the mechanics: a pressed stylus cannot draw a
curve. As scribes shifted from drawing freehand lines to pressing
wedges — faster, cleaner, more uniform — every curved outline had to
be *rebuilt* out of straight wedge strokes. The two changes together
took a few centuries; sign by sign, the pictures dissolved into
configurations of wedges that a trained hand could press in seconds.

And one you can verify yourself in chapter 04: the sign AN began as
a drawn **star**; pressed into wedges it became
<span class="script">𒀭</span> — count the strokes and the star is
still there, straightened. Not every sign kept its story legible
(you will meet KI, whose past is genuinely obscure), but the
principle is general: every classical sign is some old drawing,
turned and rebuilt in the only strokes a stylus allows.

## Why this matters for a learner

Three practical consequences follow, and the course leans on all of
them:

1. **Wedge bundles have logic.** Signs are not random scratches;
   they are rebuilt drawings. Even when the picture is lost, signs
   decompose into a countable, writable stroke order — which is why
   this course *always* teaches signs hand-first.
2. **Simplicity is real.** Some signs are one stroke; some are a
   dozen. That measurable difference — along with how often a sign
   actually occurs — is exactly how this course orders its teaching,
   as chapter 04 is about to demonstrate.
3. **Age shows.** Because forms drifted for three thousand years, a
   trained eye can date a tablet by its sign shapes alone. That
   skill, paleography, has its own place later in this school's
   catalog; in this course it is enough to know the drift is there.
