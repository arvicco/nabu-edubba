# Orientation

You are at the start of the foundations course of the cuneiform
school. It has no prerequisites. It asks only for patience and a pen.

## The promise

By the end of this course you will be able to look at signs like
these —

<p class="script sign-strip" aria-label="Three cuneiform signs">𒀭&ensp;𒆳&ensp;𒀀</p>

— and see not decoration but *writing*: a god, a mountain, water. You
will know how a few hundred wedge-shaped strokes could record
contracts, hymns, letters, and libraries for three thousand years.
You will write signs yourself, in the order a scribe would. And you
will read, unassisted, real royal inscriptions of the kings of Ur —
short formal texts that survive by the thousand, in the original
Sumerian, cited to the actual clay.

What this course does **not** do: teach you the Sumerian or Akkadian
languages. That is the work of the literacy tracks,
[102 and 103](../), which assume everything taught here. 101 is about
the writing system itself — the machine, before the languages that
ran on it.

## What cuneiform is, in three sentences

Cuneiform is the writing tradition of ancient Mesopotamia, pressed
into damp clay with a reed stylus, whose strokes are wedges — in
Latin, *cunei*, which gave the script its modern name. It was
invented before 3000 BCE for accounting and administration, grew
into a full writing system able to record language, and served
Sumerian, Akkadian, and a dozen other tongues before fading out
around the first century CE. Hundreds of thousands of clay tablets
survive — more original documents than from any other ancient
civilization — because clay, unlike paper, does not burn: fire bakes
it harder.

## The shape of the course

Chapters build strictly on one another; read them in order.

- **01–02** give you the *material* story: clay, stylus, wedge — and
  where the signs came from.
- **03** is the spine of the course: how signs carry meaning and
  sound. Read it twice.
- **04–05** put signs in your hands: a starter inventory chosen for
  frequency *and* simplicity, then the number system.
- **06** is the first summit: reading genuine seals and bricks.
- **07–09** widen the view: the scribal school, the script's three
  millennia and many languages, and how it was deciphered.
- **10** is the reference: every sign taught, every convention used.

Two commitments hold everywhere in Edubba. **Nothing untaught:** a
reading will never use a sign you have not been given — a rule our
build system enforces mechanically, not aspirationally. **Real texts:**
every reading is a genuine ancient document, cited to its source in
the [Nabu](https://arvicco.github.io/nabu/) library with its museum
identity, so you can always follow a text back to the tablet.

## How to study

- **Write everything.** A sign you have written ten times is yours; a
  sign you have only looked at is not. Pen and paper are fine; you
  are learning shapes and stroke order, not clay technique. (Chapter
  01 shows why writing *was* different on clay — and if you ever
  press a chopstick into plasticine, you will learn more in ten
  minutes than in an hour of reading.)
- **Small sessions beat marathons.** The sign inventory grows chapter
  by chapter; revisit old signs each session before meeting new ones.
- **Say the readings aloud.** Sign names and sound values stick to
  the ear faster than to the eye.
- **Use the reference early.** Chapter 10 grows with the course;
  whenever a sign feels loose, look it up.
