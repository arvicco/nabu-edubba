# The sign workshop

<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/07-of-gods-and-men/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 07</a> ended with a king taken apart into *man* and *big*. That
was not a party trick — it was a glimpse of the script's productive
machine. Scribes did not invent every sign from nothing; they
*derived* signs from signs, by a small set of moves a learner can
watch happening. This chapter hands you the workshop, and four signs
with it.

## The head you already know

Remember <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/02-from-tokens-to-signs/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02's</a> evolution figure — the drawn head that turned
on its back and dissolved into wedges? That sign has been waiting
for you since then. It is yours now:

Write it slowly the first time: the big framing wedges first, then
the interior. As you do, keep <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/02-from-tokens-to-signs/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02's</a> drawing in mind — a
profile head, quarter-turned and rebuilt in the only strokes a
stylus allows.

## Move one: mark a part — the mouth

Now the first workshop move. Take the head and hatch the region
where the mouth is: the standard sign lists describe exactly this —
the sign for "mouth" is the head-sign with strokes added at the
mouth. New meaning, minimal new drawing:

  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="SAG with added strokes yields KA">
    <span>𒊕</span><span class="op">+ strokes at the mouth →</span><span>𒅗</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>saŋ</em> "head" → <em>ka</em> "mouth": the same sign with
    the mouth region hatched. Marking a part of an old sign to name
    the part — the sign lists call variants made this way
    <em>gunû</em>-signs, "hatched" signs.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

## Move two: put one sign inside another

The second move is bolder: write one sign *inside* another and let
the container do the talking. What is water doing in a mouth?
Drinking:

<figure class="composition">
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="KA with A inside yields NAG">
    <span>𒅗</span><span class="op">×</span><span>𒀀</span><span class="op">→</span><span>𒅘</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>ka</em> "mouth" × <em>a</em> "water" → <em>naŋ</em> (nag),
    "to drink" — water written inside the mouth. Sign lists notate
    such containments KA×A: the × is literal.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

And what is a portion of food doing in a mouth? Eating — the sign
lists give the sign for "to eat" as KA with the sign GAR (a ration
or portion, not yet yours — one ▢ still owed) written inside:

## Why the workshop matters

Three moves, and you have seen two of them today (the third —
compounding two signs side by side into one word, like *e₂-gal* — you
have been doing since <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/04-your-first-signs/' | relative_url }}">chapter 04</a>):

1. **Hatch a part** (gunû): head → mouth.
2. **Contain**: mouth × water → drink; mouth × portion → eat.
3. **Compound**: man + big → king; house + big → palace.

This is how a few hundred signs covered a whole civilization's
needs without collapsing into thousands: the script reuses itself,
and once your eye knows the moves, complex signs stop being noise
and start being sentences about themselves. When the Sumerian track
hands you a monster of a sign, look for the workshop inside it.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write the chain 𒊕 → 𒅗 → 𒅘 from memory, saying *saŋ*, *ka*,
   *naŋ* — head, mouth, drink.
2. Write 𒅥 and explain its recipe to an imaginary student.
3. Review <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/07-of-gods-and-men/' | relative_url }}">chapter 07's</a> figure: which workshop move built 𒈗?
4. Look back at 𒂍𒃲 "palace" — which move is that?
