# Names that are sentences

The opening stretch closed with a dedication formula assembled from
your own inventory. This stretch opens with something stranger and
more personal: the discovery that the people on these tablets —
the farmers, overseers, and scribes of the receipts — carry names
that are complete little sentences, built from signs you already
own. First, the batch that makes it possible.

Write each five times, saying *šu*, *ma*, *igi* aloud. Two of the
three are pictures that survived the wedge; MA is the other kind of
sign — pure sound, no story — and knowing which kind you are
holding is by now an old habit.

## Grammar bite — a name is a sentence

Sumerian parents did not pick names from a list of name-words. They
composed them — a wish, a thanks, a boast, folded into a phrase and
handed to a child. The name lists of the earliest school tablets
are, sign for sign, made of the same grammar this course has been
teaching: possession, cases, the verb at the end. Which means every
name below is a one-line reading exercise.

Start with one you could have read five chapters ago:

Three signs, three words: *lugal* the king, *gal* "great," *zu*
the knowing — and the gloss's two readings differ only in how they
wire them: is the king the one who knows greatness, or the great
one who knows *you*? Names compress harder than sentences, and
editors keep both wirings open. Either way, a subject, a verb —
a sentence someone answered to. Now the new batch goes to work on
two more, from the same genre of tablets:

*Ur-* plus a god's name — "servant (literally: dog, loyal as one)
of DN" — is one of the most common name shapes in the language: you
met the god Dumuzi's servants and king Ur-Nammu's namesakes without
knowing it. MA doubles itself into the goddess's name. And *en-šu*
compresses further than editors can confidently unfold — the
translation stays honest about that.

## The eye that makes it legal

Why teach 𒅆 in a chapter about names? Because on the receipts, a
name's most frequent job is not to boast or to pray — it is to
witness. Sumerian contracts close with a list of people introduced,
each one, by the eye:

<figure class="composition">
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="igi, then a personal name — the witness formula">
    <span>𒅆</span><span class="op">+</span><span>𒈗𒃲𒍪</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    The witness formula's shape — <em>igi</em> PN: "before (the eye
    of) PN" — schematic, in the pattern the contract tablets repeat
    for each witness in turn; the name slot here reuses this
    chapter's first reading.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

"Before the eye of So-and-so": the witness's presence *is* the
legal act, and the scribes wrote it with the body part that did the
work. When a case tag joins on, you can read that too — from the
same Šuruppak archives:

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒋗, 𒈠, 𒅆 from memory; say *šu*, *ma*, *igi*.
2. Read all five readings above cold, covering translit and gloss.
3. Compose, in transliteration only, a witness line introducing
   *ur-ma-ma*: *igi* + the name. Then write it in signs — every
   sign is yours.
4. Explain in one sentence why a Sumerian name can be read at all —
   what is it made of?
