# The tablet house speaks

Every reading in this course — every receipt, proverb, votive, and
royal boast — passed through the hands of someone trained in a
school the Sumerians called *é-dub-ba-a*, the tablet house. This
chapter is about that school, and it needs exactly one new sign:
the verb the whole place existed to teach.

*Dub mu-sar* is a colophon — the line a scribe adds at the end to
say *I made this*. You have met the habit already: the modern
books in every Reference chapter carry their authors' names for
the same reason. Four and a half thousand years of title pages,
and the first ones were three signs long.

## The school with your school's name

The school itself appears in the literature by name — and the name
should look familiar, because this site is named after it:

Every sign in the school's name has been yours for chapters —
house from [101]({{ '/cuneiform/101/04-your-first-signs/' | relative_url }}),
tablet from [101]({{ '/cuneiform/101/09-the-tablet-house/' | relative_url }}),
the *-ba-a* glue from the
dedication formula. You could have read your own school's
letterhead since <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/05-a-dedication-whole/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>; now you have, off real lists.

The tablet house had a curriculum this course has been quietly
retracing: sign lists first, then proverbs by the row
(<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/14-a-row-of-proverbs/' | relative_url }}">chapter
14's</a> genre — school copies are how most proverbs survive), then
the classics: Šulgi's hymns, Gudea's temple, the laments. The
compositions known as the eduba texts — *Schooldays* and its
kin — describe the life around the copying: a boy late with his
lunch, a caning for sloppy lines, a father flattering the teacher
over dinner to improve a grade. Those texts still run on signs
ahead of your inventory; they are where this track's next course
points. But the school's *wisdom* syllabus, you can already
sample:

The pieces you can hold, piece by piece:

- **niŋ₂ … niŋ₂-e** — "a thing … the thing": <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/09-the-proverb-tablet/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09's</a>
  thing-word twice — and the second time it wears the doer's *-e*
  from <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/01-the-sentence-in-the-clay/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01</a>:
  in the second half, the *thing* is the one acting. That reversal
  is the proverb's whole engine.
- **kal-kal … kal-kal** — <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/15-i-am-the-king/' | relative_url }}">chapter 15's</a>
  "precious," doubled for intensity on both sides: treasuring,
  thoroughly, in both directions.
- **-en** — "you," closing the first half: the advice is aimed.
- The two openers, *nam-* and *me-*, are verbal prefixes from
  deeper in the chain than this course goes — the grammars carry
  them; the proverb's symmetry (you treasure it / it treasures you
  back) stands without them.

The *Instructions* are framed as a father advising his son — the
same frame as Egypt's Satire of the Trades, invented independently
a thousand kilometers away, because parents are parents. Copying
lines like this one was itself the lesson: penmanship, grammar,
and life advice in a single exercise. This chapter's drill is no
different.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒊬 from memory; say *sar*.
2. Spell "scribe," "chief scribe," and "wrote this tablet" — then
   write the colophon under today's practice lines, truthfully.
3. Write your school's name, 𒂍𒁾𒁀𒀀, from memory, and name the
   chapter that taught you each of its signs.
4. What did the tablet house teach first, and where did this
   course put the same material? One sentence.
