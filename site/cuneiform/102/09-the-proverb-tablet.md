# Let me eat, let me drink

Chapter 08 took the verbal chain apart into rings. This chapter adds
the rings that make Sumerian *wish* — and then reads the oldest
recorded version of a sentiment every student of every era has
shared at the end of a school day. First, the batch.

## Grammar bite — the wish rings

Chapter 08's prefixes reported facts: *i₃-*, *in-*, *ba-* say that
something happened; *nu-* says it did not. Sumerian has two more
rings for things that have *not* happened but should:

- **ga-** — "let me, let us": the speaker volunteers. You have owned
  the sign <span class="script">𒂵</span> since <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/02-the-case-that-hides/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02</a>; here it
  takes up its grammatical job. *ga-gu₇* — "let me eat."
- **ha-** — "may he, may it": a wish aimed outward, at someone
  else's action or at the world. This chapter's new
  <span class="script">𒄩</span> is its usual spelling. The grammars
  file these under cohortative (*ga-*) and precative (*ha-*); the
  plain sense — "let me" versus "may it be" — is all this course
  needs.

Concrete and holdable: *ga-gu₇* "let me eat" is a wish you make
about yourself; a blessing like "may the king live" runs on *ha-*.
Both rings sit exactly where <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/08-the-chain-grows-rings/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08</a> taught prefixes to sit — in
front of everything else.

## The oldest after-school sentence

Now the star reading. On an Early Dynastic tablet from the
Šuruppak school milieu — among the oldest legible literature
anywhere — a pair of lines runs:

    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒂵𒅘𒅘</span><span class="translit">ga-nag-nag</span><span class="gloss">let me drink and drink</span></div>

Every ring is one you own: *ga-* "let me," then the verb doubled —
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/03-say-it-twice/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a> doubling, meaning *again and again, thoroughly*. Mouth
(𒅗) with bread in it eats; mouth with water in it drinks — you have
known both compound signs since
[101's sign workshop]({{ '/cuneiform/101/08-the-sign-workshop/' | relative_url }}). Four and a
half thousand years later, the sentence still translates itself.

And check the citation: this is the very tablet — the same two
entries — that
[chapter 02]({{ '/cuneiform/102/02-the-case-that-hides/' | relative_url }})
read as milk names, 𒂵 the milk jug plus eat, plus drink. That
chapter told you to hold the milk loosely; here is why. Now that
you own *ga-* the ring, a second reading opens: not "milk, eat,
eat" but "let me eat and eat" — a wish, worn as a name. Which did
the parents mean? Both readings are live, and a Fara name can
carry the pun entire; editors keep them approximate for exactly
this reason. What is certain is what changed: the clay did not.
You did — and the same three signs now say more to you than they
did seven chapters ago.

## What the heart knows

Two wisdom lines to close, from the literary side — where 𒊮 does
its deepest work:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒊮𒁉𒉡𒍪</span><span class="translit">ša₃-bi nu-zu</span><span class="gloss">"its heart is unknowable" — said of a god's mind</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒃻𒀝𒍪 𒀀𒁀𒉌𒍪𒍪</span><span class="translit">niŋ₂ ak-zu a-ba i₃-zu-zu</span><span class="gloss">"the things you do — who can know them?"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Gudea's temple hymn
    (<code>urn:nabu:etcsl:2.1.7:28</code>) and a praise poem
    (<code>urn:nabu:etcsl:4.05.1:136</code>), composite texts ·
    license: ETCSL · non-commercial.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Piece by piece — first line:

- **ša₃-bi** — "its heart": this chapter's 𒊮 wearing
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/01-the-sentence-in-the-clay/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a>
  *-bi*, the "its" of things — look back for the owner, and the
  owner is a god's unknowable mind.
- **nu-zu** — "not known":
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/08-the-chain-grows-rings/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08's</a>
  *nu-* flat against the knowing 𒍪 — the exact pattern of that
  chapter's "hero unknown."

And the second:

- **niŋ₂ ak-zu** — "the things you do": the thing-maker, plus
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/02-the-case-that-hides/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02's</a>
  *ak*, "to do," plus the suffix *-zu*, "your" — the knowing-sign's
  second job that
  [chapter 07]({{ '/cuneiform/102/07-i-am-king/' | relative_url }})
  named.
- **a-ba** — "who?": the question word, bare.
- **i₃-zu-zu** — <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/08-the-chain-grows-rings/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08's</a> *i₃-* ring on a doubled *zu*: "will
  really know."

Notice 𒍪 working both its jobs in one line: suffix
"your" at the start, verb "know" at the end. Every ring, every
doubling, every case in these sentences is one this course handed
you on purpose.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒊮, 𒃻, 𒄩 from memory; say *ša₃*, *niŋ₂*, *ha*.
2. Read both proverb lines cold, covering translit and gloss.
3. Compose, in transliteration, "let me know" on the pattern of
   *ga-gu₇* — then write it in signs. (Two signs, both old.)
4. Explain the difference between *ga-* and *ha-* in one sentence
   each, with an example wish for each.
5. What are the two jobs of 𒃻 in this chapter? Name the reading
   that shows each.
