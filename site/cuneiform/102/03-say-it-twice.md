# Say it twice

Last chapter gave you the genitive — one noun folded inside another,
the case that hides a whole possessive relationship in a single
suffix. This chapter turns the other way. Instead of shrinking two
nouns into one, Sumerian sometimes sets a single word to do
more work than it did alone: to mark a plural, to intensify a
quality, or to turn a concrete thing into an abstraction. Three
signs, and one small, versatile grammar habit.

## Three new signs

Write each five times, as always, before you read on.

## Saying it twice

You have already met this device without a name. Back among
[101's first words]({{ '/cuneiform/101/04-your-first-signs/' | relative_url }}),
a reading gave you <span class="script">𒆳𒆳</span>
*kur-kur*, "all the lands" — literally *kur* "mountain, land" said
twice. That doubling was not decoration; it was the plural. Sumerian
has other ways to mark plurality, but the plainest, oldest, and
most legible one is simply to write the sign again.

Doubling does more than pluralize, though. Applied to a quality
word, the same device intensifies it:

<figure class="composition">
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="KUR doubled to kur-kur, all the lands">
    <span>𒆳</span><span class="op">→</span><span>𒆳𒆳</span>
  </p>
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="GAL doubled to gal-gal, very great">
    <span>𒃲</span><span class="op">→</span><span>𒃲𒃲</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>kur</em> "mountain, land" → <em>kur-kur</em> "all the lands"
    (doubling as plural); <em>gal</em> "big, great" →
    <em>gal-gal</em> "very great" (doubling as intensifier). Same
    device, two jobs — the reader decides which from context.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

One word, said twice, means either "more than one" or "more of it."
Context — and, honestly, practice — tells you which.

People get their own plural habit, worth naming even though this
course will not drill it yet: nouns for persons often take a suffix
*-ene* instead of doubling — *lu₂-ene*, "people, men" (plural), from
*lu₂* "man." The sign that spells *-ene* is not yet in your
inventory, so for now just recognize the shape in transliteration
when you meet it; the doubling you can already write.

## Turning things into ideas

The third habit this chapter teaches is a prefix, not a suffix, and
it does something grammar rarely lets a single sign do: it changes a
word's part of speech. Put <span class="script">𒉆</span> *nam-* in
front of a concrete noun and you get the abstraction built from it —
not "king" but "the state of being king":

<figure class="reading reading--script reading--composed">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒉆𒈗</span><span class="translit">nam-lugal</span><span class="gloss">nam-lugal — "kingship," built from nam- + lugal "king"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    A compound, not a citation — assembled from signs you already hold,
    to show the machine at work: concrete noun in, abstraction out.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

This is the same *nam-* you meet at the head of the fate-goddesses'
names and in the word for destiny itself, generalized into a
production line: give it almost any concrete noun and it will hand
you back the idea of it.

## Reading: a name built on knowing

Fara-period name lists again, and for the first time a name whose
translation openly admits its own uncertainty — which is, honestly,
the normal condition of these readings, only usually left unsaid:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒀭𒀀𒍪</span><span class="translit">dingir-a-zu</span><span class="gloss">Diŋir-azu — a name built on "god" and "knowing" (approximate; perhaps "the god knows the water/seed")</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>ArOr</em> 39, p. 14, ED IIIa (ca. 2600–2500 BCE), Šuruppak
    (mod. Fara)?, collection unlocated.
    <a href="https://cdli.earth/artifacts/10008">CDLI P010008</a>
    via <a href="https://arvicco.github.io/nabu/">Nabu</a>,
    <code>urn:nabu:cdli:p010008</code> · license: attribution. Keep
    the hedge in view — name translations of this age are proposals,
    not certainties, and this one more than most.
  </figcaption>

Three signs you can now all name — god, water, the knowing sign —
and a meaning that scholarship offers only cautiously. That caution
is not a failure of the field; it is what four and a half thousand
years of distance actually looks like up close.

## Reading: a whole sentence, as a name

Compare that hedge with this one, from the same name list you first
met back in orientation — a name that is not a compound of nouns at
all, but a complete little sentence:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒈗𒈾𒉆</span><span class="translit">lugal-na-nam</span><span class="gloss">Lugal-nanam — "he is indeed king" (a name; na-nam is the affirmative "it is indeed", as the grammars describe)</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>OIP</em> 104, 014, ED IIIa (ca. 2600–2500 BCE), Oriental
    Institute, Chicago.
    <a href="https://cdli.earth/artifacts/10063">CDLI P010063</a>
    via Nabu, <code>urn:nabu:cdli:p010063</code> · license:
    attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Sit with that for a moment: an entire affirmation, a whole assertion
about the world, packed into a single personal name — and every
sign in it is already yours. Someone, four and a half thousand years
ago, named a child a sentence.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒉆, 𒁕, 𒍪 from memory; say *nam*, *da*, *zu*.
2. Double <span class="script">𒆳</span> and
   <span class="script">𒃲</span> in your head and give both
   meanings each doubling can carry — plural, and intensified.
3. Build *nam-lugal* in signs from memory, and say what the prefix
   does to any noun it is attached to.
4. Compose *dumu-da*, "with the child," and name the case this
   chapter added to your kit.
