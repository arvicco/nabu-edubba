# Build it for me

Chapter 11 read a ruler's dedication and left two rings of its
final verb politely unopened — *mu-na-du₃*, "built it for her,"
carried "an opener and a 'for her' whose full anatomy a later
stretch opens." This is that stretch, and this chapter opens the
anatomy. Three signs first, all three from the world of votive
gifts.

Write each five times: *du₃*, *ru*, *ama*.

## Grammar bite — the last two rings

Chapter 08 named the chain's openers *i₃-*, *in-*, *ba-* and the
wishes *ga-*, *ha-*. Two rings remained, and they are the two the
dedications run on:

- **mu-** — an opener like *i₃-* and *ba-*, but with a direction:
  it points the action *home* — toward the speaker, the here, the
  gift being handed over. The grammars give its full theory a
  chapter of its own; "the homeward opener" is the working grip
  this course needs.
- **-na-** — "for him, for her": the dative you have known since
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/02-the-case-that-hides/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02's</a> *-ra*, folded *inside* the verb. Sumerian says the
  receiver twice — once on the noun, once in the chain — and the
  chain's copy is *-na-*. You have already watched it ride by: the
  unnamed ring in <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/08-the-chain-grows-rings/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08's</a> *i₃-na-ba* was exactly this.

## The god who wanted his house

The building verb's most famous scene is a dream. Gudea, ruler of
Lagash, dreamt that his god Ninŋirsu stood over him with a demand
— and the hymn writes the demand with <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/03-say-it-twice/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a> oldest trick,
saying it twice:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒈠𒆕𒈾 𒈠𒆕𒈾</span><span class="translit">ma-du₃-na ma-du₃-na</span><span class="gloss">"build it for me, build it for me!" — the god, twice, in a dream</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Gudea's temple hymn. <code>urn:nabu:etcsl:2.1.7:228</code> ·
    license: ETCSL · non-commercial.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Look at *ma-* and enjoy the economy: it is the homeward opener
*mu-* with "for me" fused straight into it — when the receiver is
the speaker, Sumerian squeezes both rings into one syllable. A god
says "build it *for me*" in exactly two signs, and repeats himself
the way anyone does at the door of someone slow to get up. The
whole rest of Gudea's hymn — the longest Sumerian composition we
have — is him getting up.

## Another ruler, another gift

Votives survive by the hundred, and the formula holds across all
of them. From a dedication of a ruler of Uruk:

*A mu-na-ru* — literally "water/offering he-hither-for-him-
dedicated"; idiomatically, "dedicated this to him." Thousands of
objects end in exactly these four signs. From today, no votive in
any museum case closes with a sentence you cannot read.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒆕, 𒊒, 𒂼 from memory; say *du₃*, *ru*, *ama*.
2. Parse *mu-na-du₃* aloud, ring by ring, then do the same for
   *a mu-na-ru*.
3. Why can the god's demand say "for me" in one syllable? One
   sentence.
4. Re-read <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/11-a-dedication-for-real/' | relative_url }}">chapter 11's</a> full inscription. Count what is still
   boxed, and notice what no longer is.
