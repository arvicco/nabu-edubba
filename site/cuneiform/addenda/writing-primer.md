# The writing primer — the scribe's hand

The courses of this school teach you to *read*. This page teaches
the other half of the craft: how the signs were physically
*written* — which wedge a scribe pressed first, and how we can
possibly know. It serves every course and belongs to none; come
back to it whenever your hand wants more than your eye.

One promise up front, because this topic needs it: everything
below is labeled. Some of it is **proven from the clay**, some of
it is **honest modern reconstruction**, and the difference will
always be stated. No ancient text ever wrote the rules down — they
lived in the schoolroom, hand to hand, and survive only in the
impressions themselves.

## Three wedges, one stylus

Every sign decomposes into three basic marks:

<div class="glyph-exhibit">
  <figure><span class="script">𒀸</span><figcaption>horizontal — head at the left</figcaption></figure>
  <figure><span class="script">𒁹</span><figcaption>vertical — head at the top</figcaption></figure>
  <figure><span class="script">𒌋</span><figcaption>the corner wedge (Winkelhaken)</figcaption></figure>
</div>

The mechanics are simpler than they look. The stylus's main edge
is dropped into the clay and "will always correspond to the main
axis of the wedge": the deep, wide **head** is where the edge bit
in, the tapering **tail** is where it eased out. Passing between
wedge types "is all a matter of varying the stylus' orientation to
the tablet and the angle at which it meets the surface" — the
stylus rotates a quarter-turn between a vertical and a horizontal,
and the corner wedge is the tip's corner alone, dipped and
lifted.[^cwt] Obliques are the same wedges at a tilt.

Practice them the way the tablet house did: **rows of single
wedges before any sign** — verticals, then horizontals, then
corner wedges, in series, until they come out even.[^cwt] Only
then turn to proper signs. (A chopstick and a slab of plasticine
remain the best modern classroom; an eduba text even preserves the
teacher's clay recipe — "take the clay, knead it, flatten it, mix
it, roll it like a ball, make it thick, make the tablet!")

## How anyone knows the order

Here is the beautiful part. When a scribe pressed a wedge that
crossed an earlier one, the new impression pushed a little clay
into the old one, or bent its tail. The damage is directional:
the *later* wedge always cuts the *earlier* one. Look closely
enough at enough signs, and the clay replays the scribe's hand,
wedge by wedge, five thousand years later.

This overlap evidence is the foundation of everything on this
page. Surveying it across the corpus, Jon Taylor of the British
Museum found that **the order of wedges within a sign was highly
standardized** — fixed for most signs from the Old Babylonian
period (the early second millennium BCE) and followed routinely by
scribes until the last days of cuneiform, some fifteen hundred
years later. Only the Early Dynastic period — the age of the Fara
tablets the courses read — still shows real variability, the
system not yet settled.[^taylor] **Status: proven from the clay.**

## The rules the clay confirms

Two general principles hold across the standardized script:

- **Rows of horizontals go top to bottom** — the upper wedge
  before the lower.
- **Within a row, wedges go left to right.**

Both are stated by the scholars who did the surveying, with the
overlaps behind them.[^taylor] **Status: attested general
tendency** — real, but with exceptions, and never the whole story.

Modern teachers extend these into a fuller working kit: verticals
left to right; diagonal runs bottom to top; in signs that open
with a vertical over a horizontal, the vertical first. These
extensions are **honest reconstruction** — generalized from
practice and from the attested tendencies, not each proven wedge
by wedge. How honest? Two current handbooks disagree: one orders a
stack of horizontals top-down, the other, for alphabetic cuneiform,
bottom-up.[^wright] Where the clay has not been surveyed, order is
convention, and conventions differ.

## A convention learned sign by sign

Now the fact that makes wedge order genuinely deep. You might hope
the rules above *generate* the right order for any sign. They do
not — and one pair of signs proves it:

<div class="glyph-exhibit">
  <figure><span class="script">𒉺</span><figcaption>PA — vertical first, then the horizontals</figcaption></figure>
  <figure><span class="script">𒄑</span><figcaption>GIŠ — horizontals first, then the vertical</figcaption></figure>
</div>

PA and GIŠ are nearly the same shape. On the same first-millennium
tablets, scribes wrote them in **opposite orders** — the overlaps
say so plainly.[^taylor] The order was not geometry; it was part
of each sign's identity, learned in school along with the sign
itself, the way Chinese, Japanese, and Korean writers learn a
canonical stroke order for every character today — a comparison
Taylor himself draws. A scribe's hand knew each sign as a little
choreography, not a picture.

And because the choreography was taught, it betrays the teacher.
Deviations from the standard order help scholars date tablets and
distinguish scribal schools — sometimes individual hands. At least
one sign, <span class="script">𒆠</span> KI, demonstrably
*changed* its canonical order between periods: the same shape,
re-choreographed.[^ur3] **Status: proven from the clay**, sign by
surveyed sign — but the survey is young, explicitly preliminary,
and **no complete per-sign order chart exists**, ancient or
modern.

## How to practice, honestly

1. **Drill the three wedges in rows** before any sign — the
   ancient school's own sequence, and still the fastest way to a
   steady hand.
2. **Follow the attested principles**: top row before bottom, left
   before right, and let verticals run left to right.
3. **Copy real sign forms** from the courses' tables and readings,
   and keep whatever order you fall into *consistent* — that is
   what the ancient standard was: not a law of nature, but a
   school's shared habit, kept for fifteen hundred years.
4. **Do not hunt for the canonical chart.** There isn't one. The
   scribes never wrote their rules down; the clay remembers what
   the school never stated.
