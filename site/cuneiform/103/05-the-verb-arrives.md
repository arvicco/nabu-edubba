# The verb arrives

Every law on the stele is a tiny story: someone *did* something,
and now something *shall happen*. Akkadian carries that whole
machinery in the shape of its verbs — no pronoun needed, no helper
words: <span class="norm">iddi</span> is already "he charged,"
<span class="norm">inaddin</span> is already "he shall pay." This
chapter gives you the two shapes that matter and the three signs
the laws spell them with.

## Two shapes of time

Old Babylonian runs its laws on two verb shapes:[^verbs]

<table class="sign-table sign-table--tail-fit">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Shape</th><th>Says</th><th>On the stone</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>preterite</td><td>a done deed, told past</td><td><span class="norm">iddi</span> "he charged" · <span class="norm">ubbir</span> "he accused"</td></tr>
    <tr><td>durative</td><td>what shall now happen</td><td><span class="norm">inaddin</span> "he shall pay" · <span class="norm">iddâk</span> "he shall be killed"</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Two small words ride along everywhere. **-ma**, glued to a verb's
tail, means "and (then)" — it chains a law's events in order.
**lā** is the negation of record: <span class="norm">lā uktīn</span>,
"he did *not* convict." Now read the laws' verbs off the stone,
one at a time:

A note of honesty for the grammar-curious: Akkadian verbs also
come in derived stems — <span class="norm">ubbir</span> and
<span class="norm">uktīn</span> are "thickened" forms with their
own nuances of meaning. A later chapter names that system
properly; for now, read each verb as the story-piece it is, and
let the shapes accumulate.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒌑, 𒌒, 𒊌 from memory; say <em>u</em>, <em>ub</em>,
   <em>uk</em>.
2. Preterite or durative: <span class="norm">iddi</span>?
   <span class="norm">inaddin</span>? What does each one say
   about time?
3. Spell <em>la uk-ti-in-šu</em> in signs. Which sign carries
   the "him"?
4. What does -ma do to a law's narrative? One sentence.
