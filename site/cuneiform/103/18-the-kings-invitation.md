# The king's invitation

Near the stele's end, Hammurapi stops legislating and does
something stranger: he addresses YOU. Not the courts, not the
gods — the future person standing before the stone with a
grievance and, crucially, someone who can read. This chapter
answers him.

been waiting four thousand years to say to you:

Item by item, one last time:

- <span class="norm">awīlum ḫablum ša awātam iraššû</span> —
  "the wronged man who has a case": the wronging-verb of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/07-the-strong-and-the-weak/' | relative_url }}">the creed</a>
  come back as a man; the verb of having wears
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/17-silver-changes-hands/' | relative_url }}">chapter 17's</a>
  quiet ša-clause -u.
- <span class="norm">ana maḫar ṣalmīya</span> — "before my
  image": <span class="norm">maḫar</span> is a construct doing
  a preposition's work — from the same root as
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/16-eye-tooth-bone/' | relative_url }}">chapter 16's</a>
  meḫrum, "equal": what stands FACING you.
- <span class="norm">lillikma … lišmēma … līmur …
  linappišma</span> — the whole passage runs on the precative,
  the may-it-be form the
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/12-say-to-him-a-letter/' | relative_url }}">letters</a>
  taught you: let him come, let him hear, let him see, let it
  breathe. A king wishing, not ordering — because the reader
  he imagines is not his subject. It is whoever can read.
- <span class="norm">lištassima</span> — "let him have (it)
  read aloud": the reading-verb with a -t- of its own — one of
  the lexical -t- builds the grammars keep beyond this course;
  met, named, left at the frontier. Reading was done aloud,
  and mostly by hired eyes: the king assumes a scribe at the
  wronged man's side.
- <span class="norm">dīnšu līmur</span> — "let him see his
  case": the seeing-verb, closed by your last syllable-sign.
  But you no longer need the hired eyes. That is the whole
  point of the school.
- <span class="norm">libbašu linappišma</span> — "let his
  heart breathe": Akkadian's word for relief is a lung filling.
  The stele's promise to the man who can read it.




## Drill before you go

1. Write 𒅈, 𒌫, 𒀩 from memory; say <em>ar</em>,
   <em>ur</em> — and for the statue, say
   <em>ṣalmum</em>.
2. Recite the invitation's last four lines from the signs
   alone, aloud. You are the reader the king imagined.
3. Fill the empty cells of the
   <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/19-reference/' | relative_url }}">Reference's</a>
   tense-and-stem grid with the model root p-r-s — then check
   yourself against a grammar someday. The course's cells are
   all you need to read what you have read.
4. <span class="norm">libbašu linappišma</span> — parse every
   piece, then say it to yourself in your own language.

*This closes Cuneiform 103 — every sign and every form now
stands on the
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/19-reference/' | relative_url }}">Reference</a>
shelf. May your heart breathe.*
