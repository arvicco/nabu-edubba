# Say to him: a letter

The stele is Akkadian in its Sunday clothes. The other half of
Old Babylonian literacy is the **letter** — clay the size of a
bar of soap, carried in a clay envelope, opening with a formula
every schoolchild drilled. Today you read real mail.

## The frame every letter wears

A Babylonian letter does not begin "Dear X." It begins with an
order — to the *tablet*, or to the messenger who will read it
aloud: *to X, speak! — thus (says) Y.* The reader is the
sender's voice; writing is recorded speech. Here is the frame
on a real school tablet, with the school's own gods in both
names:

Item by item — the frame is new grammar, so take it slowly:

- <span class="norm">ana … qibīma</span> — "to …, speak!": the
  speaking-verb as an **imperative** — the naked command form,
  no prefix at all — with the -ma of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
  sealing it. The addressee is grammatically the *hearer* of
  the tablet.
- <span class="norm">Sîn-ašarēd</span> — "Sîn-is-foremost": the
  moon-god's name is written 𒂗𒍪 — two signs you have known
  for a year, EN and ZU, fused by convention into one divine
  name — behind the god-star of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/12-reference/#sign-1202D' | relative_url }}">101</a>.
- <span class="norm">umma … -ma</span> — "thus (says) …": the
  quotation-opener. Everything after this word, to the end of
  the tablet, is direct speech. Old Babylonian has quotation
  marks; they are just made of clay.
- <span class="norm">aššumīya</span> — "for my sake": aššum,
  "because of," wearing this chapter's new sign — -ya, "my,"
  the first-person cousin of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/01-sound-by-sound/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a>
  -šu.
- <span class="norm">šanat šār</span> — "a 3600th year," and the
  green shows you why the mouth and the tablet part ways: the
  writing is MU ŠAR₂.KAM — MU the year-sumerogram (the same sign
  is Sumerian "name" and "year"; Babylon reads it
  <span class="norm">šattum</span>, "year of"
  <span class="norm">šanat</span>), ŠAR₂ the myriad, KAM the
  counting tag. A blessing does not
  say "long life"; it says *year number 3600* — may you outlive
  arithmetic.

## And what did he want?

Butter, honestly — the body of this school letter is a dowry
list. Real letters keep the same shape and sharper wants. One
sender, mid-negotiation about two kegs of beer, closes with the
finest conditional sentence in the corpus — read it again, at
home in its mailbag at last
(<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a>
met it as a stem specimen):

Four thousand years of correspondence have not improved on that
sentence either. Note what it does with your kit:
<span class="norm">šumma</span> from the laws, the durative for
an ongoing state, the imperative from this chapter's frame —
the whole letter-writer's toolbox in eight signs.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒅀, 𒊹, 𒄰 from memory; say <em>ia</em>, <em>šar</em>,
   <em>kam</em>.
2. Open a letter to your own teacher: ana … qibīma, umma …-ma —
   fill in the names, out loud.
3. Whose voice does the tablet speak in, and to whom is qibīma
   actually addressed?
4. What is MU doing in MU ŠAR₂.KAM — which language is written,
   and which is spoken?
