# The noun wears its case

Sumerian marked a noun's role with little tags at the end of the
whole phrase — you spent half of 102 learning to spot them. An
Akkadian noun does something your Sumerian eyes will find almost
familiar and your ears completely new: it *inflects*. The word
itself changes its ending for its role in the sentence, the way
Latin or Russian nouns do. Three endings run the show, and this
chapter teaches you to see all three.

## The three endings

An Old Babylonian noun in the singular wears one of three
endings (the plural has its own table, in
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/08-more-than-one/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08</a>):[^mimation]

<table class="sign-table sign-table--tail-fit">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Role</th><th>Ending</th><th>Sounds like</th><th>You will read it in</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>subject (nominative)</td><td><em>-um</em></td><td>𒌝</td><td><span class="norm">šarrum</span>, "the king" — <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00's</a> very first spelling</td></tr>
    <tr><td>object (accusative)</td><td><em>-am</em></td><td>𒄠</td><td><span class="norm">rubâm</span>, "the prince" — below</td></tr>
    <tr><td>after "of" / a preposition (genitive)</td><td><em>-im</em></td><td>𒅎</td><td><span class="norm">mātim</span>, "of the land" — below</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

All three end in the same closing hum: **-m**. The grammars call
it mimation, and it is the single most Old Babylonian thing about
Old Babylonian — later stages of the language quietly dropped it.
When you hear a text close its nouns with -um, -am, -im, you are
hearing Hammurabi's century.

## The name on the stone

Now the payoff. The stele's prologue introduces its king with a
personal-name determinative — <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/12-reference/#sign-12079' | relative_url }}">101's tally-stroke</a> 𒁹, which in
Akkadian documents stands silently before a person's name the way
𒀭 stands before a god's. Read what follows it, sign by sign, out
loud:

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒄠, 𒁴, 𒍢 from memory; say <em>am</em>, <em>tim</em>,
   <em>ṣi</em>.
2. Recite the three case endings with their jobs. What do all
   three share?
3. Spell the king's name in signs, determinative included, without
   looking.
4. <em>ina mātim</em> — why does the noun end in -im here and not
   -um? One sentence.
