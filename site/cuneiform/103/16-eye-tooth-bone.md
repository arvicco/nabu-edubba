# Eye, tooth, bone

<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/11-an-eye-for-an-eye/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 11</a>
gave you the bone law in sound alone and made a promise: when
the bone-word's own signs arrive, these four lines will owe you
nothing new. The signs arrive now — and then the stele does
something the famous quotations never mention: it walks the
same injuries down the social ladder, and flesh becomes silver.

Together, then: 𒄊𒉻𒁺 — three signs, one word,
<span class="norm">eṣemtum</span>, "bone." The scribes write it
GIR₃.PAD.RA₂ and no one ever says those syllables; the reader
speaks the Akkadian word whole, exactly as
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/10-the-river-decides/' | relative_url }}">the River</a>
taught you.

Every piece is old: <span class="norm">ištebir</span> is

The promise is kept: the law owes you nothing.

Item by item:

- <span class="norm">šinni awīlim</span> — "the tooth of a
  man": <span class="norm">šinnum</span>, the tooth, in the
  construct — and spelled with
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/03-lord-of-heaven-and-earth/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a>
  eye-sign-turned-sound, ši. A tooth written with an eye.
- <span class="norm">meḫrišu</span> — "his equal": the word
  <span class="norm">meḫrum</span> is the hinge of the whole
  famous doctrine. Tooth for tooth holds BETWEEN EQUALS — the
  stele says so in one word, and almost no one quotes it.
- <span class="norm">ittadi</span> — "he has knocked out": the
  charging-verb of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
  (<span class="norm">iddi</span>), now in the perfect — the
  root's own n and the tense's -t- pressed together into -tt-.
- <span class="norm">šinnašu inaddû</span> — "his tooth they
  shall knock out": the court's plural durative, the same
  verdict-voice as <span class="norm">išebbirū</span> above.

## The scale of persons

Laws 196, 197 and 200 answer flesh with flesh. The three laws
between and after them answer flesh with SILVER — because the
victim stands lower on the ladder. Their signs include the
commoner's and the slave's title-words, which this course
leaves on the shelf, so hear them as sound — every verb
already yours:

- **Law 198** — <span class="norm">šumma īn muškēnim uḫtappid
  ū lū eṣemti muškēnim ištebir — 1 mana kaspam išaqqal</span>:
  "If he has blinded a COMMONER's eye or broken a commoner's
  bone — one mina of silver he shall weigh out."
- **Law 199** — <span class="norm">šumma īn warad awīlim
  uḫtappid ū lū eṣemti warad awīlim ištebir — mišil šīmišu
  išaqqal</span>: "If he has blinded a man's SLAVE's eye or
  broken his bone — half his price he shall weigh out."
- **Law 201** — <span class="norm">šumma šinni muškēnim ittadi
  — šalušti mana kaspam išaqqal</span>: "If he has knocked out
  a commoner's tooth — a third of a mina he shall weigh
  out."[^scale]

Three ranks, three answers: the awīlum's body is answered in
kind; the muškēnum's in fixed silver — a mina is sixty
shekels, and by the shekel's own wage-math that is years of a
worker's pay; the slave's in his market price, because the law
sees property where it saw a person. And the verb of every
verdict is <span class="norm">išaqqal</span>, "he shall WEIGH
out": there are no coins yet — silver is money by the scale,
payment by the balance-pan.

## Two, exactly

One last debt, the oldest on the books:
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/02-the-noun-wears-its-case/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02</a>
promised that the dual would come. Here it is, one row wide:
for things that come in natural pairs, Akkadian keeps a number
beyond singular and plural — <span class="norm">īnān</span>,
"the two eyes"; <span class="norm">šēpān</span>, "the two
feet." By Hammurapi's day it is already a survival, kept for
pairs and little else — which is why the laws you just read
write <span class="norm">īn</span>, the plain singular, even
for an eye that has a twin. A number fading out of the
language, caught here in its last natural home: the body.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒄊, 𒉻, 𒄴 from memory. For the first two say
   nothing alone — put 𒁺 after them and say
   <em>eṣemtum</em>; for 𒄴 say <em>aḫ</em>, <em>eḫ</em>,
   <em>uḫ</em>.
2. Spell the bone-word in signs, from memory, and say what the
   reader says.
3. <span class="norm">iddi</span> — <span class="norm">ittadi</span>
   — <span class="norm">inaddû</span>: one root, three forms.
   Name each, in
   <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/15-it-has-happened/' | relative_url }}">chapter 15's</a>
   melody.
4. Who pays what: the eye of an awīlum? the bone of a
   muškēnum? the tooth of an equal? Answer from the scale.
5. <span class="norm">īnān</span> — what number is that, and
   why do the laws still write <span class="norm">īn</span>?
