# A script crosses a border

Everything you have read so far was Sumerian — a language with no
known relatives, written by the people who invented the script for
it. This course reads **Akkadian**: a Semitic language, cousin to
Hebrew and Arabic, with different sounds, different word-shapes,
and a completely different grammar.[^huehnergard] And yet you will
read it in exactly the wedges you already know, because around the
middle of the third millennium BCE the scribes of Akkad and Babylon
did not invent a script — they borrowed one whole.

What survives a borrowing like that, and what changes? The signs
survive: their shapes, their names, their order of strokes. What
changes is the *work* they do. Sumerian leaned on word-signs —
𒈗 was the word "king." Akkadian's words inflect and shift too much
for that, so its scribes leaned the other way: they took each
sign's *sound* and used it as a bare syllable, spelling their own
words piece by piece. The king of Babylon is *šarrum* — written
*ša-ru-um*, three signs, three syllables, no "king"-sign in sight.

## Two layers of writing it down

From here on, examples come in two forms, and the course will
always keep them straight. **Transliteration** is what the tablet
actually spells, sign by sign, hyphenated: <em>ša-me-e</em>.
**Bound transcription** is the reconstructed Akkadian word those
signs spell, written joined-up with its vowel lengths marked:
<span class="norm">šamê</span>, "of heaven." The first is evidence;
the second is interpretation — useful, standard, and always one
step removed from the clay. A bound transcription never appears in
this course without the spelling it came from.

## What your Sumerian years already bought

Here is the remarkable part, measured: of every syllable written in
the Old Babylonian documents this course draws on, **more than half
are spelled with signs you already know**.[^coverage] Sumerian
values like <em>a</em>, <em>na</em>, <em>ma</em>, <em>šu</em>,
<em>ta</em>, <em>ba</em>, <em>ra</em>, <em>bi</em>, <em>la</em>
pass into Akkadian unchanged — same sign, same sound, new language
around it. Two of the very first words:

Not every sign crosses the border with the same value it had, and
some cross with *new* values — when a familiar sign returns with
an Akkadian reading, its table row will say **veteran**, and its
glyph will link to its Akkadian page. Sign lore for this course
lives on its own shelf, the
[Akkadian Addenda]({{ '/cuneiform/addenda-akk/' | relative_url }}),
kept deliberately separate from the
[Sumerian shelf]({{ '/cuneiform/addenda/' | relative_url }}):
one sign, two lives, two pages.

## Where this course is going

The destination is a real monument. Around 1750 BCE, Hammurabi,
king of Babylon, set up a stone stele carrying almost three hundred
laws between a prologue and an epilogue — the **Codex Hammurapi**,
the most famous Akkadian text in existence. Its opening words are
<em>i-nu an ṣi-ru-um</em>, "when lofty An…" — and by chapter 04
you will read them in the original, every sign yours. By chapter 06
you will read its first law. This is not a specimen course: it is a
straight march to the stone.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒊭, 𒅇, 𒌝 from memory; say <em>ša</em>, <em>u</em>,
   <em>um</em>.
2. Spell <em>ša-me-e</em> in signs without looking, then say the
   bound transcription it spells.
3. Which of the two prepositions above means "in"? Write it.
4. In one sentence: what did Akkadian scribes keep when they
   borrowed the script, and what did they change?
