# The stems named

Twice now this course has met a verb, called it "thickened," and
asked you to wait —
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
for <span class="norm">ubbir</span> and
<span class="norm">uktīn</span>,
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/08-more-than-one/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08</a>
for <span class="norm">ukinnū</span>. The waiting ends here.
Akkadian builds every verb from a consonant skeleton — the
consonantal root
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a>
introduced — and each way of building has a name.

## Four ways to build a root

Take the giving-verb you have known since
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a>:
<span class="norm">inaddin</span>, "he shall give." Strip away
the "he" prefix and the tense-vowels, and a skeleton of three
consonants remains: **n-d-n**, "give." That skeleton is the
consonantal root, and every Akkadian verb has one. A **verbal
stem** is a fixed way of building flesh back onto the skeleton
— and Old Babylonian uses four. The grammars name them with
single letters;[^stems] each letter stands for something you
can see on the clay and hear in your mouth.

Now watch two roots go through all four builds, side by side.
The grammars demonstrate on **p-r-s**, "to divide — to decide a
case," and drill on **m-ḫ-ṣ**, "to strike" — both verbs at home
in a law collection — and so will we. This is pure transcription
— no signs yet, nothing to spell, just the sounds: keep your eye
on the three consonants of each root, which never move, while
the identical build lands on both. All the forms say "he …-ed":

    <tr><td><strong>Š</strong> — the causative</td><td><strong>š-</strong> in front</td><td><span class="norm">u<strong>š</strong>apris</span></td><td><span class="norm">u<strong>š</strong>amḫiṣ</span></td><td>"he MADE (someone) decide · strike"</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>N</strong> — the passive</td><td><strong>n-</strong> in front, melting in</td><td><span class="norm">i<strong>pp</strong>aris</span></td><td><span class="norm">i<strong>mm</strong>aḫiṣ</span></td><td>"it WAS decided" · "he WAS struck"</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Four names, and each name says why. **G** is "ground" because
every other build stands on it: the verb doing its plain
dictionary deed, nothing added. The **D** doubling means
thoroughness — the same deed done piece by piece, made formal;
and see how it lands on whatever consonant sits in the middle:
-rr- in one root, -ḫḫ- in the other. The **Š** form does not do
the deed at all: it makes someone else do it. If that idea
feels slippery, English has it too — it just needs a different
word each time: the tree *falls*, the woodsman *fells* it
(makes it fall); the child *eats*, the nurse *feeds* her (makes
her eat). Akkadian does all of that with the one š-. And in the
**N** build the subject is done-to — and the n- refuses to
stand before the root's first consonant, melting into it: -pp-
in one root, -mm- in the other. A doubled FIRST consonant, no n
in sight, is the passive's footprint.

Say each column aloud: <span class="norm">iprus</span>,
<span class="norm">uparris</span>,
<span class="norm">ušapris</span>,
<span class="norm">ipparis</span> — then
<span class="norm">imḫaṣ</span>,
<span class="norm">umaḫḫiṣ</span>,
<span class="norm">ušamḫiṣ</span>,
<span class="norm">immaḫiṣ</span>. Two skeletons, the same
four garments.

## Now the verbs you own

Hold the grid and place the real verbs from your readings onto
it — each root spelled first, then the build it wears:

- **n-d-n**, "give," and **a-l-k**, "go" — bare skeleton, "he"
  prefix, nothing else: <span class="norm">inaddin</span> "he
  shall give" and <span class="norm">illak</span> "he goes"
  sit in the **iprus** slot and its durative — everything
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
  taught. (<span class="norm">iddi</span> "he charged" lives
  there too, on **n-d-i** — a root whose weak tail trims the
  form.)
- **a-b-r**, "accuse" — its middle consonant is **b**, and the
  D build says it twice: <span class="norm">ubbir</span>, the
  **uparris** slot, and the clay writes the doubling out in
  full: <em>u-ub-<strong>bi</strong>-ir</em> — -bb- where
  uparris has -rr-. Not casual blame; a formal accusation.
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/08-more-than-one/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 08's</a>
  <span class="norm">ukinnū</span>, "established," is the same
  slot on **k-w-n**, "be firm": that root's middle consonant is
  a weak w that slips away, so the doubling lands on the last
  consonant instead — the -nn- is right there in the spelling.
- **w-b-l**, "carry" — **š-** put in front:
  <span class="norm">šūbilam</span>, "send it here!," the
  **ušapris** slot. The root's first consonant is a weak w that
  the vowels close over, so ušapris's shape surfaces as
  <span class="norm">šūbil-</span>; but the š- in front is
  still the entire story: *make-carry*. And making someone
  carry a thing to you is, in one word, sending for it. (The
  plain G of the same root, <span class="norm">itabbal</span>
  "he carries off," walks through the exhibits just below.)
- **d-w-k**, "kill" — **n-** put in front, melting in:
  <span class="norm">iddâk</span>, "he shall be killed," the
  **ipparis** slot. The root's middle w is weak (it survives
  only as the long â), and the melted n- survives as the
  doubled -dd-, exactly as -pp- and -mm- in the grid: the verb
  flipped from doing to being-done-to, and law 1's verdict
  stands in it. And here the whole system rests on one
  doubling: the plain G is <span class="norm">idâk</span>, "he
  kills" — say them side by side, <span class="norm">idâk</span>,
  <span class="norm">iddâk</span> — one held d is all that
  stands between killing and being killed.

And each build still tells time the way
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
taught — a preterite for the done deed, a durative for what
shall happen. <span class="norm">Ubbir</span> is the D
preterite; <span class="norm">iddâk</span> the N durative. And
<span class="norm">uktīn</span>? A D stem with a -t- slipped
inside — the grammars build a whole wing for those infixes;
this course names them where met and moves on.

## Two verbs, walking

The next chapter reads a law that travels — a man walks to the
river and the river decides. Its two verbs of motion are yours
today, and both end on the hoe:

Item by item:

- <span class="norm">šumma</span> — "if," straight off the
  stele — the law-word doing daily work, from
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/06-if-a-man/' | relative_url }}">chapter 06</a>.
- <span class="norm">tarammanni</span> — "you love me": the
  loving-verb in the G durative, ta- "you" in front, -anni "me"
  on the tail — the suffix family of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/01-sound-by-sound/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a>
  -šu, now in the first person.
- <span class="norm">šūbilam</span> — "send (it) here!": the
  carrying-verb again — the same root as
  <span class="norm">itabbal</span> above — but built in the
  Š stem: *cause it to be carried*. An order, aimed at the
  reader, with the "hither" -am on the end.

One root, walking in the G and shipped in the Š, in a law and in
a letter — that is the whole stem system in one afternoon. The
letter itself, frame, greeting and all, is three chapters away.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒅋, 𒀠, 𒋰 from memory; say <em>il</em>, <em>al</em>,
   <em>tab</em>.
2. G, D, Š, or N: <span class="norm">iddâk</span>?
   <span class="norm">ubbir</span>? <span class="norm">illak</span>?
   <span class="norm">šūbilam</span>? Spell each root, then say
   what the letter is doing to it.
3. One root carries both <span class="norm">itabbal</span> and
   <span class="norm">šūbilam</span>. What changed between them,
   and what stayed?
4. Spell <em>i-il-la-ak</em> in signs, from memory.
