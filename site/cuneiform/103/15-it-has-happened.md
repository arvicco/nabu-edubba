## The third tense

Akkadian has one more tense, and it does not say merely "it
happened." It says "it **has** happened — and here we stand":
the deed done, and still on the table. The grammars call it the
perfect,[^perfect] and its signature is a **-ta-** slipped in
right after the root's first consonant. Watch it join the two
tenses you own, on the
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">model roots</a>
— pure transcription first, as ever:

Say the new row aloud: <span class="norm">iptaras</span>,
<span class="norm">imtaḫaṣ</span>. Same skeleton, same "he"
prefix — only the little -ta- of the record, pressed in behind
the first consonant.

## The melody you have been reading all along

Why a third tense? Listen to how a law talks. In the if-clause,
the deeds pile up in the preterite; the LAST deed — the one
that seals the case — takes the perfect; then the verdict
speaks in the durative. You have been reading that melody since
law 1:

- <span class="norm">ubbir</span> — "he accused": preterite,
  the deed;
- <span class="norm">lā uktīnšu</span> — "he has NOT convicted
  him": perfect, the proof that seals it;
- <span class="norm">iddâk</span> — "he shall be killed":
  durative, the sentence.

Deed, proof, sentence. And now
<span class="norm">uktīn</span> itself, the word
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
called "thickened" and
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a>
left half-named, opens like a puzzle-box: it is the
establishing-verb in the D stem — doubling hidden in the weak
root, as
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a>
warned — AND in the perfect, the -ta-
squeezed thin: u-k-**t**-īn. Every piece of it something you
now own. The same is true across your readings:
<span class="norm">uḫtappid</span> "he has blinded"
(<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/11-an-eye-for-an-eye/' | relative_url }}">chapter 11</a>)
is a D perfect with its -ta- in plain sight;
<span class="norm">iktašassu</span> "it has overwhelmed him"
and <span class="norm">ištalmam</span> "he has come out safe"
(<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/10-the-river-decides/' | relative_url }}">chapter 10</a>)
are G perfects, the -ta- colored by each root's vowel.

your drill grid steps off the page into the corpus. A man
strikes the cheek of his better; the assembly answers:

Item by item:

- <span class="norm">lēt awīlim</span> — "the cheek of a man":
  <span class="norm">lētum</span>, the cheek, stripped to its
  construct to bind with the next noun —
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/03-lord-of-heaven-and-earth/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a>
  build, on a body part.
- <span class="norm">ša elīšu rabû</span> — "who is above him":
  the hinge-word ša opening a description, and
  <span class="norm">eli</span> — "over" — doing comparison's
  work: over-him great = greater than he. Rank, in three words.
- <span class="norm">imtaḫaṣ</span> — "he has struck": the
  perfect of the striking-root, -ta- visible between the m and
  the ḫ, and the new bear-sign closing it. The deed of record.
- <span class="norm">ina puḫrim</span> — "in the assembly":
  <span class="norm">puḫrum</span> is where the city's elders
  sit; the sentence is carried out before every eye.
- <span class="norm">1 šūši immaḫḫaṣ</span> — "sixty he shall
  be struck": <span class="norm">šūši</span> is sixty, the
  scribes' round number — the same sixty that still divides
  your hour — and the verb is the SAME root flipped passive in
  the durative: n- melted in, middle doubled.
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 09</a>
  built <span class="norm">immaḫiṣ</span> as an exercise; the
  stele pays it out in real strokes.

One root, full circle: <span class="norm">imtaḫaṣ</span> the
deed, <span class="norm">immaḫḫaṣ</span> the sentence — G
perfect at the top of the law, N durative at the bottom, sixty
lashes apart.

   pressed <em>aṣ</em>.

   <span class="norm">imḫaṣ</span>,
   <span class="norm">imaḫḫaṣ</span>,
   <span class="norm">imtaḫaṣ</span> — say what each build
   did.
3. <span class="norm">uktīn</span> — stem, tense, and why its
   doubling hides.
4. Spell <em>im-ta-ḫa-aṣ</em> in signs, from memory.
5. Law 1's chain — <span class="norm">ubbir</span>,
   <span class="norm">uktīn</span>,
   <span class="norm">iddâk</span>: name each verb's tense and
   hear the melody. Deed, proof, sentence.
