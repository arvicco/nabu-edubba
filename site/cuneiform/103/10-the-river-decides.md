# The River decides

Law 1 dealt with a murder charge that could not be proven; the
court's answer was to kill the accuser. Law 2 asks a harder
question — what if the charge is **sorcery**, where no witness
can exist? — and hands the case to a judge no one can bribe.

## A god you can wade into

The stele writes the River as 𒀭𒀀𒇉 — the divine
determinative, then water, then the deep — and the last two
signs are read as ONE word: <span class="norm">Id</span>, the
River's name. Neither sign is sounded on its own; water-plus-deep
IS the name, a picture of a river speaking as a river.

So the one writing wears three labels, and they never compete —
hold the chain once and every line below reads itself:

- **ENGUR** is the deep-sign's NAME on the shelf — its identity,
  never spoken in this course;
- **ID₂** is how the pair 𒀀𒇉 is written in transliteration —
  a word-sign in capitals with its little index, the same habit
  as E₂;[^idfiling]
- **Id** is what your mouth says: the god's name, declined like
  any noun.

Name, spelling, voice — in that order, every time the River
flows through a line. This is the **ordeal**: the accused walks
into the god and the god gives a verdict no appeal can touch.
Every sign of the phrase is now yours, and so is the whole law.

The new machinery, item by item:

- <span class="norm">kišpī / kišpū</span> — "sorcery": a noun
  that only exists in the plural, like
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/08-more-than-one/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08's</a>
  nišū — first as object (-ī), then as subject (-ū) of…
- <span class="norm">nadû</span> — "(is) laid": the
  stative again
  (<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/08-more-than-one/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08</a>),
  now masculine plural — charges lie on a man the way a burden
  does.
- <span class="norm">Id</span> — the divine River: on the page
  {d}ID₂, the determinative and then 𒀀𒇉, water-plus-deep
  spoken as one name — and declined like any noun. The law
  walks a man into a god.
- <span class="norm">išalliamma</span> — "he shall plunge,
  and": the durative of plunging, with the hither -am you met
  on <span class="norm">šūbilam</span> in
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a> —
  the motion aimed at the river, then -ma chaining the verdict.
- <span class="norm">iktašassu</span> — "it has overwhelmed
  him": the overwhelming-verb with the -t- infix
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a>
  named — here it marks the deed as done and decisive; the
  grammars call this shape the perfect. The final -šu, "him,"
  melts against the verb into -ssu.
- <span class="norm">bīssu itabbal</span> — "he shall carry off
  his estate": E₂, the house-sign from
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/12-reference/#sign-1208D' | relative_url }}">101</a>,
  as a sumerogram wearing an Akkadian suffix — bītum + -šu →
  bīssu, the same melting as above.

The mirror's own pieces:

- <span class="norm">šuāti</span> — "that (man)": the pointing
  word, riding after its noun in the accusative.
- <span class="norm">ūtebbibaššuma</span> — "has cleared him":
  the purifying-verb, doubled-middle build with the -t- infix —
  a
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a>
  D at full formality. Its second sign is the stretch's one
  remaining box.
- <span class="norm">ištalmam</span> — "he has come out safe":
  šalāmum, "to be whole," in the perfect — the same root the
  whole Near East still greets with.
- <span class="norm">bīt mubbirīšu</span> — "the estate of his
  accuser": the construct chain of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/03-lord-of-heaven-and-earth/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03</a>,
  built on the doer-noun of
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/06-if-a-man/' | relative_url }}">chapter 06</a>.

Read the whole law once more, aloud, both halves. A man walks
into a god carrying an unprovable accusation, and walks out —
one way or the other — with the case closed. Four thousand
years on, the law still knows this problem; it has simply run
out of rivers.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒇉, 𒁍, 𒅅 from memory; say <em>bu</em> and
   <em>ig</em> — and for the deep-sign say nothing alone: put
   water before it and say <em>Id</em>.
2. Spell the River — determinative, water, deep — and say its
   Akkadian name.
3. <span class="norm">iktašassu</span> and
   <span class="norm">ištalmam</span> share a shape. What does
   the -ta- say that a plain preterite would not?
4. Who takes whose estate, in which verdict? Answer from the
   endings, not from memory.

[^idfiling]: The sign lists file this pair-value both ways —
    id₂ and i₇ name the same writing, water + deep — and the
    corpus transliteration of the stele uses the latter
    (<code>{d}i7</code>, raw ATF). This course writes ID₂ so
    that the written word and the spoken word agree.
