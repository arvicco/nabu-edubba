# If a man…

Nearly three hundred laws stand on the stele, and almost every one
of them opens with the same word: <span class="norm">šumma</span>,
"if." Then comes a man, a deed, and a consequence in the durative.
This chapter teaches the "if" and the "man" — and then reads law 1
from the top.

## The legal person

<span class="norm">awīlum</span> — spelled <em>a-wi-lum</em> —
is the stele's word for a free man in full legal standing; the
laws weigh compensation differently for an
<span class="norm">awīlum</span> than for anyone else, which
makes this one noun a keyhole view of Babylonian society. In the
accusative (chapter 02!) it is <span class="norm">awīlam</span>,
<em>a-wi-lam</em> — and that little -lam is the one sign this
chapter has not taught you. Watch what that does to the reading
below: one honest box, in the second word of the law.

## Law 1, from the stone

Read it aloud, all seven lines. The rhythm is the law's own:
if-clause piling up deeds with -ma, then the two-word verdict.

"If a man accused a man, and laid a murder charge against him,
and did not convict him — his accuser shall be killed." The
stele's very first ruling is about false accusation: the law
begins by protecting the accused. Four thousand years have not
improved on the priority.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒋳, 𒉿, 𒈝 from memory; say <em>šum</em>, <em>wi</em>,
   <em>lum</em>.
2. Spell <span class="norm">awīlum</span>, then
   <span class="norm">awīlam</span> in transliteration. Which
   sign changes, and why?
3. Which sign in <em>ne-er-tam</em> is the veteran, and what
   were its other two readings back in 101?
4. Recite law 1 in transliteration, glancing at the signs only
   when stuck.
