# Silver changes hands

The laws say what SHOULD happen. Here is a tablet where it
DID: a loan from Sippar, written a generation before Hammurapi
was king. It is small, complete, and utterly ordinary — money,
interest, a borrower, a deadline, witnesses, a date — and that
ordinariness is the point. This is what most cuneiform IS.

Item by item — and watch the languages:

- <span class="norm">1 mana kaspim</span> — "one mina of
  silver": the silver-word is
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/07-i-am-king/#sign-121AC' | relative_url }}">102's</a>
  pure-metal compound crossing whole — written KU₃.BABBAR,
  spoken <span class="norm">kaspum</span> — under
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/12-reference/#sign-12313' | relative_url }}">101's day-sign</a>
  doing its "white" duty.
- <span class="norm">MAŠ₂ … GIN₂ uṣṣab</span> — the rate: on
  the mina, a third of a shekel "will GROW" —
  <span class="norm">uṣṣab</span>, the adding-verb in the
  durative. Interest as the herdsman's arithmetic: the flock
  bears kids, the silver bears shekels.
- <span class="norm">Sîn-imittī ša ina Ša-ibdî wašbu</span> —
  the borrower, pinned to his village. The verb wears a quiet
  final -u that verbs take inside a ša-clause — the grammars'
  subjunctive; met here, named, and left for the frontier.
- <span class="norm">KI … ŠU BA-AN-TI</span> — "from Zablum …
  he has received": and this is not Akkadian. The frame of the
  contract is SUMERIAN — the notary's formulary, five hundred
  years senior to the borrower —
  and your
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/08-the-chain-grows-rings/' | relative_url }}">102</a>
  reads it as grammar, not incantation: šu … ti, "took in
  hand," with the chain's ba-an- in the middle.
- <span class="norm">ūm kinūni</span> — the deadline: the
  midwinter brazier-festival, when the braziers are lit. No
  calendar dates; the year turns by festivals.
- <span class="norm">I₃-LA₂-E</span> — "he shall weigh out":
  Sumerian again, the formulary's twin of the laws'
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/16-eye-tooth-bone/' | relative_url }}">išaqqal</a>
  — two languages, one balance-pan.

- The year has no number. Babylon dates by the **year-name**:
  each year is named for a royal deed, and this one — "the year
  Immerum built Inanna's temple" — is how the tablet tells you
  WHEN. Scribes kept lists of these names; those lists are how
  we order their history today.
- 𒀭𒂗𒍪 — the moon-god's name, and a scribal wink: written
  EN.ZU with
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/101/12-reference/#sign-12097' | relative_url }}">101's lord-sign</a>
  and
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/03-say-it-twice/#sign-1236A' | relative_url }}">102's knowing-sign</a>,
  but READ backwards: Suen — Sîn. Even god-names play the
  rebus game.
- <span class="norm">IGI</span> — "before": the witness is an
  eye. The sign's
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/06-names-that-are-sentences/#sign-12146' | relative_url }}">oldest job</a>,
  doing courtroom duty. And the first witness's name is a whole
  sentence — "Sîn heard me" — built on the hearing-verb you
  will meet again in the last chapter's blessing.
- A man named Sîn-imittī witnesses a loan taken by a man named
  Sîn-imittī: the same name serving another man. Sippar had
  many; the tablet does not blink.

## Two languages, one tablet

Step back and look at what you just read. The LIFE of the
document is Akkadian — the growing interest, the borrower and
his village, the festival. The FRAME is Sumerian — KI, ŠU
BA-AN-TI, I₃-LA₂-E, MU — a notary's formulary already five
centuries old, kept exactly as
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">102</a>
taught it to you, long after Sumerian left the streets. Dead
languages keep working: ask any diploma written in Latin. You
now read both layers, and the green tells you — sign by sign —
which is which: where the capitals are Akkadian
(<span class="logo">KASPIM</span>, <span class="logo">ŪM</span>),
a sumerogram is being voiced; where they are Sumerian
(<span class="logo">ŠU BA-AN-TI</span>), the old formulary
itself holds the pen.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒈧, 𒂆, 𒈹 from memory; say <em>maš</em> — and for
   the other two, say what the reader says:
   <em>šiqlum</em>, <em>Ištar</em>.
2. Spell the interest line and say it whole. Whose arithmetic
   is a kid goat?
3. ŠU BA.AN.TI — whose grammar is that? Parse it with your
   <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/102/08-the-chain-grows-rings/' | relative_url }}">102</a>.
4. <span class="norm">uṣṣab</span> and
   <span class="norm">išaqqal</span> — one grows, one weighs:
   name each verb's language and tense.
5. Date this tablet aloud, the scribes' way.
