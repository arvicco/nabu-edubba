# More than one: the plural

<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/02-the-noun-wears-its-case/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 02</a>
taught the noun in the singular and said so out loud; the hedge
comes due today. The stele rarely speaks of one verdict or one
person — it speaks of *the verdicts of justice* and *the people*,
and this chapter gives you their endings.

## The endings, doubled

An Old Babylonian noun in the plural drops the
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/02-the-noun-wears-its-case/' | relative_url }}">mimation</a>
and wears one of these:

<table class="sign-table sign-table--tail-fit">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Who</th><th>Ending</th><th>You will read it in</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>masculine, subject</td><td>-ū</td><td><span class="norm">šarrū</span> "kings" · <span class="norm">awīlū</span> "men"</td></tr>
    <tr><td>masculine, everything else</td><td>-ī</td><td><span class="norm">šarrī</span> · <span class="norm">nišī</span> "of the people"</td></tr>
    <tr><td>feminine, subject</td><td>-ātum</td><td><span class="norm">šīmātum</span> "destinies" · <span class="norm">awâtum</span> "words"</td></tr>
    <tr><td>feminine, everything else</td><td>-ātim</td><td><span class="norm">šīmātim</span> · <span class="norm">awâtim</span></td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Three honest notes. First: in the plural the genitive and the
accusative give up their quarrel — one ending, -ī or -ātim,
covers both, which is why the table says "everything else."
Second: some masculine nouns take the feminine-looking plural —
<span class="norm">dīnum</span>, "verdict," becomes
<span class="norm">dīnātum</span>, "verdicts"; the dictionary
decides, not the logic. Third:
<a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/07-the-strong-and-the-weak/' | relative_url }}">chapter 07's</a>
<span class="norm">nišī</span>, "of the people," now has a home —
a plural-only noun, standing in the second row.

## The verdicts of justice

The epilogue names the whole monument in one plural construct
chain. Read it from the stone:

Take it apart, item by item:

- <span class="norm">dīnāt</span> — "verdicts-of": a PLURAL in
  the construct state.
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/03-lord-of-heaven-and-earth/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 03's</a>
  rule holds exactly: the ending (-ātum) is stripped for binding,
  and what remains reaches for the next noun.
- <span class="norm">mīšarim</span> — "of justice," genitive
  singular, closing the chain — and opening on this chapter's
  night-sign as pure [mi].
- <span class="norm">ša</span> — the hinge:
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00's</a>
  "which," turning the whole clause that follows into a
  description of the verdicts.
- <span class="norm">Ḫammurapi</span> — the royal name you have
  spelled since
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/02-the-noun-wears-its-case/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02</a>,
  behind its {diš} name-marker.
- <span class="norm">šarrum lē'ûm</span> — "the able king":
  noun and adjective agreeing in the nominative, standing in
  apposition to the name.
- <span class="norm">ukinnūma</span> — "established, and": the
  verb that made the verdicts stand. It is one of the
  "thickened" shapes
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/05-the-verb-arrives/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a>
  promised to name —
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/09-the-stems-named/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09</a>
  keeps that promise.

Reassembled, literally: "the verdicts of justice which Ḫammurapi,
the able king, established." You have just read the monument's
own name for itself — the phrase scholarship abbreviates as "the
Code" is, on the stone, a plural.

## The king counts his words

Two lines later the epilogue turns boast, and the boast runs on
feminine plurals:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒀀𒉿𒌅𒌑𒀀 𒈾𒀾𒂵</span><span class="translit">a-wa-tu-u-a na-as-qa</span><span class="gloss"><span class="norm">awâtūya nasqā</span> — "my words are choice"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒅁𒊺𒌅𒌑𒀀</span><span class="translit">ep-še-tu-u-a</span><span class="gloss"><span class="norm">epšētūya</span> — "my deeds…"</span></div>
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𒊭𒉌𒉆</span><span class="translit">ša-ni-nam</span><span class="gloss"><span class="norm">šāninam</span> — "…a rival…" — and here the sentence holds its breath</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Codex Hammurapi</em> (composite text), epilogue.
    <code>urn:nabu:cdli:p464358:a:3336'</code>–<code>:a:3338'</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Item by item:

- <span class="norm">awâtūya</span> — "my words":
  <span class="norm">awâtum</span>, the feminine plural of the
  word for "word," wearing -ya, "my" — the same possessive
  family as
  <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/01-sound-by-sound/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a>
  -šu, "his."
- <span class="norm">nasqā</span> — "are choice": an adjective
  doing a verb's job with no verb in sight — the grammars call
  this the **stative**, and the -ā is its feminine-plural voice,
  agreeing with the words it praises. This chapter's curse-sign
  carries the as, mid-word and innocent.
- <span class="norm">epšētūya</span> — "my deeds," the same
  build again: feminine plural, -ya. The corner-sign opens it
  as ep.
- <span class="norm">šāninam</span> — "a rival," accusative,
  waiting for its verb… which sits in the NEXT line of the
  stele, and which you cannot read for one more sign. The
  boast completes at the stretch's end, where the king finally
  says who he is.

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒈪, 𒀾, 𒅁 from memory; say <em>mi</em>, <em>as</em>,
   <em>ib</em>.
2. Decline aloud: one king, kings (subject), of the kings. Then:
   one destiny, destinies, of the destinies.
3. <span class="norm">dīnāt mīšarim</span> — why does the first
   word carry no case ending at all? Which chapter's rule is at
   work?
4. Spell <em>mi-ša-ri-im</em> in signs, from memory.
