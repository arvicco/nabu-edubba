# When lofty An…

Four chapters of preparation end here. This page reads the first
words carved on the stele of Hammurabi — not an adaptation, not a
simplified school text: the monument's own opening, in its own
signs. One new sign is all it still needs from you.

## The stone speaks

Three lines, read aloud. The stele opens with a "when…" that will
hold its breath for three hundred more lines before the "then"
arrives — monumental Akkadian loves a long suspense — and the
"when" itself is two veterans standing together:

Notice what the three lines quietly demonstrate. Line 1 is pure
syllable-spelling. Line 3 is pure Sumerian heritage, untouched.
And line 2 is the mixture that makes Akkadian cuneiform what it
is: a sumerogram read in Akkadian (*šar*, construct state — the
missing -um is <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/03-lord-of-heaven-and-earth/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a> signal), a silent classifier, and a
name spelled out sound by sound. Every skill this course has
taught you, in one line of stone.

## Why "when"?

Because the prologue is one enormous sentence: *When* An and Enlil
gave Marduk his rank and Babylon its kingship — *at that time*
(the resolution arrives hundreds of lines later) *they named me,
Hammurabi, to make justice rise in the land.* The laws hang from
that sentence like the stele's own colophon. You now hold its
first breath; the rest of the stretch works toward the clause
where the king finally says <span class="norm">anāku</span> —
"I am he."

## Drill before you go on

1. Write 𒉣 from memory; say <em>nun</em>.
2. Spell <em>i-nu an ṣi-ru-um</em> in signs, unaided, and give
   its bound transcription.
3. In line 2, which sign is silent, which is a sumerogram, and
   how do you know the sumerogram is in the construct state?
4. Why does <span class="norm">ṣīrum</span> end in -um and
   <a href="{{ '/cuneiform/103/02-the-noun-wears-its-case/' | relative_url }}">chapter 02's</a> <span class="norm">ṣīram</span> in -am? One
   sentence.
