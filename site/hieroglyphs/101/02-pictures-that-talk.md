A round-bottomed jar cannot stand on its own; Egyptian households
propped theirs on rings like this one. Draw it — a squat ring seen
from the side — and notice, once more, the pattern from <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a>:
a piece of kitchen furniture, borrowed to spell the sound *g*,
nothing jar-like about it. Why pictures behave this way is exactly
this chapter's story.

A picture of an owl is just an owl. Writing begins at the moment a
picture stops meaning what it shows and starts meaning what it
*sounds like*. This chapter is about that moment on the Nile.

## Tags on jars

The oldest Egyptian writing we can point at comes from a tomb at
Abydos known as U-j, from around 3250 BCE — as early as anything
from Mesopotamia. It is not literature. It is inventory: small
labels of bone and ivory, each pierced with a hole for a cord, each
carved with a few signs and once tied to goods — oil, cloth,
deliveries from named estates.

Sound familiar? It should. Cuneiform's story starts the same way —
counting first, language later. Writing was not invented to record
poetry. Poetry moved in later, the way people move into a house
built for the shop downstairs.

## The king with a catfish for a name

By about 3100 BCE the trick is unmistakable. A great ceremonial
palette — a slab of carved slate celebrating a king who unified
Egypt — writes its royal name at the top with two pictures: a
**catfish** and a **chisel**. In Egyptian, catfish is *nꜥr* and
chisel is *mr*. Put the sounds together: *Nꜥr-mr* — **Narmer**.

The pictures have stopped showing and started *spelling*. Narmer is
not the fish-and-chisel king; the fish and the chisel are borrowed
purely for their sounds, exactly as if English wrote "Carson" with a
drawing of a car and a drawing of a sun. This borrowing — the
**rebus** — is the engine inside every early writing system, and
Egypt's version of it is the direct ancestor of the signs you will
learn in <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/04-your-first-signs/' | relative_url }}">chapter 04</a>: an owl that means nothing owlish, only the
sound *m*; a mouth that has stopped being about mouths and become
the sound *r*.

One more detail worth noticing: Narmer's name sits inside a frame —
a rectangle representing the royal palace facade, called a *serekh*.
The habit of putting a king's name in a protective enclosure is as
old as Egyptian kingship itself; in <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/05-names-in-rings/' | relative_url }}">chapter 05</a> you will meet its
famous successor, the ring-shaped cartouche, and read real names
inside it.

## From names to sentences

For its first few centuries, Egyptian writing stays terse: names,
titles, labels, year-records — language compressed to tags. Whole
grammatical sentences appear in the Old Kingdom, and by the Middle
Kingdom (around 2000 BCE) the script carries everything: teachings,
stories, hymns, medical recipes, tax rolls, love songs. The system
you will learn in the next chapter is the mature machine — but every
part of it, sound-signs, word-signs, and silent classifiers, is
already visible in embryo on a slate palette five thousand years
old.[^origins]

   the chisel — then invent one for your own name in your own
   language.
2. Why do the earliest written objects record deliveries and counts
   rather than stories?
3. What is a serekh, and what later device plays its role?
