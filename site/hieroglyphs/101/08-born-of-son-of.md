# Born of, son of

Chapter 07 kept pointing at little signs "shadowing" bigger ones
and promising an explanation. Here it is — and it turns out to be
one of the most humane ideas in the whole script: Egyptian spelling
checks itself. Three new signs first, all three chosen because the
tomb walls use them to say the oldest sentence families know:
*this is my child*.

Draw them. The duck is the charmer of the three: give it its
upturned tail and it is unmistakably a pintail.

## Grammar bite — spelling that checks itself

Here is the trick chapter 07 showed you without naming. A scribe
writes 𓏠 *mn* — and then adds 𓈖 *n* right after it. Not because
the word has three sounds; it has two. The extra 𓈖 is a
**phonetic complement**: a one-sound sign repeating part of what
the big sign already said, so the reader can confirm the reading
at a glance. It is spelling with a built-in checksum.

Why bother? Because many signs look alike at chisel size, and
because some signs carry more than one value. The complement
settles it: whatever this sign might be elsewhere, *here* it ends
in *n*. Egyptians read with training wheels permanently attached —
and it made their writing astonishingly robust. You already met the
same spirit in chapter 03's "belt and suspenders" spellings; now it
has a name and a system.

The 𓋴 adds no new sound — *ms* already ends in *s*. It simply
underlines it. From now on, when a reading gives you "too many"
letters, check for a complement before you blame the scribe.

## The tomb wall as family album

Egyptian tombs are covered in portraits with captions, and the
captions are a genre of exactly two words plus a name: *his son
So-and-so, his daughter So-and-so*. You can now read the genre in
full:

Read the middle line again and enjoy what is happening: a father
named his daughter after Khufu's mother, and four and a half
thousand years later you caught it, because you can read. Bebi and
Teti carry royal names too. The tomb wall is a family album, and
the captions never went out of style: *his son, his daughter,
beloved, of his body*.

## A queen's pot

The little pot goes to work inside another grand name — this one
borne by Khufu's own queen and by namesakes ever after:

Count the layers: a two-sound sign, a complement confirming half of
it, and a feminine *-t* — the whole system of this chapter in one
word. The complement habit is why hieroglyphic spelling, which
looks so ornamental, is actually redundant in the engineering
sense: knock one sign out, and the word usually still reads.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓄟, 𓅭, 𓏌 from memory; say *ms*, *zꜣ*, *nw*.
2. Explain a phonetic complement in one sentence to an imaginary
   student, using 𓏠𓈖 as your example.
3. Read the three family captions cold, covering the gloss column.
4. Write "his daughter, Hetep-heres" from memory — duck, t, f, and
   the queen's name.
5. Find the complement in *Ḥn,wt≡sn* and say what it confirms.
