# Two sounds in one sign

Twenty-six one-sound signs, three royal names, and a whole offering
formula: that was the opening act. If Egyptian stopped there, a
scribe would spell every word letter by letter, forever, like a
typist who never learns the shortcuts. Egyptian did not stop there.
Most signs in a real inscription carry *two* consonants in one
shape — and with this chapter's three, some of the most common
words on any monument fall open.

Draw all three. The game board rewards a close look: the little
pieces standing on top are what make it 𓏠 and not just a table.

## How two-sound signs behave

A biliteral is not an abbreviation and not a syllable — it is two
consonants fused in one shape, vowels still nobody's business,
exactly as <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/03-how-signs-mean/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03</a> warned. The reflex to build is simple: when
you see 𓏠, hear *m-n*; when you see 𓎟, hear *n-b*; when you see
𓁷, hear *ḥ-r*. One-sound signs still do the fine work between
them — you will see them shadowing the biliterals in a way <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/08-born-of-son-of/' | relative_url }}">chapter
08</a> will name properly. For now, read through it.

Start with a man's name, four and a half thousand years old:

Say it: *m-n*, then *n* again, then *j*. Meni. Tradition gives that
same name — Menes — to the first king ever to rule all Egypt.
*This* Meni was no pharaoh; he was somebody's neighbor with a nice
tomb. Egyptians named their sons after great kings for five
thousand years, and so does everyone else to this day.

## A queen's name, read whole

Now both remaining new signs, plus an old friend from the offering
formula, in one of the grandest names of the Old Kingdom:

Hetepheres is the name of Khufu's own mother — the queen whose
hidden tomb furniture is a treasure of the Cairo museum. This one,
on a private tomb wall, is a namesake; the name itself, "her face
is at peace," is a small poem either way. Notice 𓏏 and 𓊪 tucked
after the offering-table sign, and 𓂋 after the face: one-sound
signs shadowing bigger signs' sounds. <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/08-born-of-son-of/' | relative_url }}">Chapter 08</a> explains why the
scribes doubled up like that; for now, trust the word.

## Lord of the sentence

The basket earns its keep in real grammar. From a Late Period
biography — a career promise compressed to four signs' worth of
words:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓇋𓅱 𓎡 𓂋 𓎟</span><span class="translit">jw≡k r nb</span><span class="gloss">"you will become a lord" — jw (it is so) + ≡k (you) + r (toward) + nb (lord)</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Late Period biographical inscription (BBAW historical-
    biographical texts), via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawhistbiospzt:KZXWVSR44ZGPBCXDK3EVYVRNIQ:IBUBdWK2QfwsfUQHmLv7fWjUAP8</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Four words, a whole future. The little *r*, "toward," does the work
English does with "become": you are *heading to* lordship. And 𓎟
at the end is the destination — the same basket that, on temple
walls, means "every": *nb tꜣwy*, lord of the Two Lands; *nṯr nb*,
every god. One sign, both jobs, context deciding — <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/03-how-signs-mean/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03's</a>
oldest lesson still running.

## The hidden one

Three signs you have owned since <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00</a> — reed, game board,
water — now spell one of the biggest names in Egypt:

<figure class="composition">
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="j plus mn plus n spelling Amun">
    <span>𓇋</span><span class="op">+</span><span>𓏠</span><span class="op">+</span><span>𓈖</span><span class="op">=</span><span>𓇋𓏠𓈖</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Jmn</em> — Amun, "the hidden one." Composed here from taught
    signs in the standard spelling; you will meet him carved in real
    titles soon enough. The final 𓈖 shadows the 𓏠 — <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/08-born-of-son-of/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08's</a>
    story, one page early.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Amun of Thebes: the god whose name rode inside Amenhotep and
Tutankh**amun**, the god whose temple at Karnak is still the
largest religious building ever raised. You can now write his name
with three signs you learned before you could read a single word.
That is what biliterals buy: reach.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓏠, 𓎟, 𓁷 from memory; say *mn*, *nb*, *ḥr*.
2. Read all three readings cold, covering the gloss column.
3. Write *Jmn* from memory, then check it against the composition
   above.
4. 𓎟 means "lord" in one phrase and "every" in another — invent
   an English sentence where one word does two jobs like that, and
   notice you never once got confused.
