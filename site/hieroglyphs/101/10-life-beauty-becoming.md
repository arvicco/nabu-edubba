# Life, beauty, becoming

Every museum gift shop sells at least two of this chapter's signs
as jewelry. That is not a reason to skip them — it is a reason to
learn what they actually say, because the pendants never tell you.
Four signs, three sounds each, and between them half the big ideas
Egypt carved about itself.

Two of the four carry an honest question mark on their pictures —
the strap and the windpipe are the standard identifications, not
certainties, and this course tells you so rather than selling you
a story. What is certain is what they write.

## The sculptor who gives life

Start with the working world. In the Amarna tomb lists, the
sculptor's job title is two signs long, and it is a theology in
miniature:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓋴𓋹</span><span class="translit">sꜥnḫ</span><span class="gloss">"sculptor" — literally "one who makes live": the causative s- in front of ꜥnḫ, life</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Amarna tomb inscriptions (BBAW <em>Amarna</em>), via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawamarna:Y2HZBORZEVBELK7FCF5B37I3U4:IBUBd7qytTen0EjxuszCxZlWTcQ</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Put an *s* before a verb and Egyptian makes it causative: *ꜥnḫ*,
to live; *s-ꜥnḫ*, to make live. The workshops applied that word to
the man who carves a statue — because a statue, to an Egyptian,
was not a portrait but a body a spirit could actually use. The
job title takes the ankh literally: the sculptor is a life-giver
with a chisel.

## Khufu lives

The family-album genre of <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/08-born-of-son-of/' | relative_url }}">chapter 08</a>, one more time — now with a
name that smuggles in the grandest builder of them all:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓅭 𓆑 𓈖 𓄡𓏏 𓆑 𓐍𓅱𓆑𓅱𓋹</span><span class="translit">zꜣ≡f n ẖ,t≡f Ḫwfw-ꜥnḫ</span><span class="gloss">"his son of his body, Khufu-ankh" — the name means "Khufu lives"; the Great Pyramid's builder, worn as a first name</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Old Kingdom tomb inscriptions (BBAW <em>Grabinschriften</em>),
    via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawgrabinschriften:4HIWQSJTPFDJTNCBC6NB6LJKBA:IBUBd7Qj4zWUVE3cjukP9MHhCH4</code>
    · license: attribution. Sign inventory is the source's own;
    order follows the word.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

A man named "Khufu lives," presumably by parents who worked for
the royal house — walking proof of how a king's name outlived him
in the streets, not just on the pyramid. Sound it: *ḫ-w-f-w*, then
the ankh. You have read the name of the man who built the Great
Pyramid, inside the name of somebody's son.

## When good goes bad

The scarab's verb, in the wild — a line of wisdom literature that
could have been written this morning:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓃀𓏲𓄤𓆑𓂋 𓆣𓂋 𓅓 𓃀𓏲𓃀𓇋𓈖𓅪</span><span class="translit">bw-nfr ḫpr m bw-bjn</span><span class="gloss">"the good becomes the bad" — nfr with its complements f and r; the scarab turning it; and "bad" closed by <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/09-the-silent-signs/' | relative_url }}">chapter 09's</a> sparrow, right on schedule</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Middle Kingdom literary papyrus (SAW <em>Literatur</em>), via
    Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:sawlit:DKAFDVSXYFADJDS3Z3AOISAP4U:IBUBd9O7vMHlG0vRhdNBQlfEEiw</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Every layer of the last three chapters is in that one line:
biliterals and triliterals doing the heavy lifting, complements
double-checking them, a classifier filing "bad" under *sparrow*.
And the sentiment — good things curdling — is why Egyptians wrote
wisdom literature in the first place. Reading it is the point of
the whole course.

## The good god

The flag and the nefer meet in the most standard royal epithet
there is:

<figure class="composition">
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="netjer plus nefer — the good god">
    <span>𓊹</span><span class="op">+</span><span>𓄤</span><span class="op">=</span><span>𓊹𓄤</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>nṯr nfr</em> — "the good god": composed here from taught
    signs in the standard spelling; it stands before kings' names
    on monuments beyond counting, and private people wore it too —
    the tomb lists know a man simply named <em>Nṯr-nfr,w</em>.
    Adjective after its noun — Egyptian's standard order, worth
    fixing in your ear now.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Why "good god" and not "great god"? Because *nfr* is bigger than
"good": complete, functioning, beautiful the way a well-made thing
is beautiful. The epithet claims the king *works* — a quality
control stamp, at throne scale.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw all four signs from memory; say *ꜥnḫ*, *nfr*, *ḫpr*,
   *nṯr*.
2. Read the sculptor's title and explain the causative *s-* in one
   sentence.
3. Read the wisdom line cold, then name one complement and one
   classifier inside it.
4. Whose name hides inside *Ḫwfw-ꜥnḫ*, and what does the whole
   name say?
5. The ankh's picture is uncertain but its meaning is not — say
   why that distinction matters, in one sentence.
