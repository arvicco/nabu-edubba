# How signs mean

This is the chapter the whole course stands on. Egyptian writing
runs on three kinds of sign — signs for **sounds**, signs for
**words**, and silent signs that **classify**. Every inscription you
will ever read is these three, mixed. Read this chapter twice.

## One picture, three jobs

Here is a single sign, the ground-plan of a house:

<p class="script sign-strip" aria-label="The house sign">𓉐</p>

Watch it do all three jobs in turn.

**Job one: a word-sign.** Write 𓉐 with a small upright stroke 𓏤
beside it — the stroke is the script's way of saying "this sign
means the thing it shows" — and you have written the word *pr*,
"house". One picture, one word. Egyptologists call this a
**logogram** (or ideogram).

**Job two: a sound-sign.** The word for "go out" happened to sound
like *pr* too. So the scribes borrowed the house — chapter 02's
rebus, working exactly as it did for Narmer's catfish — and wrote
"go out" as 𓉐𓂋𓂻. The house here contributes only its *sound*,
p-r. Nothing in "go out" is about houses. Signs used this way are
**phonograms**.

**Job three belongs to the last sign in that spelling.** The walking
legs 𓂻 are not pronounced at all. They stand at the end of the word
and whisper to the reader: *this word is about motion*. That silent
classifier is called a **determinative**, and Egyptian uses them
constantly — words for motion end in walking legs, words for
speaking and eating end in a man with his hand to his mouth 𓀁,
divine names end in a god, place names end in a town plan. In a
script with no spaces and no vowels, determinatives are how a reader
knows where words end and which of several look-alike words they
have met.

And the middle sign, the mouth 𓂋? That is a sound-sign too — it
reads *r*, and it is there to *reinforce* the r-sound already inside
*pr*, a habit called a phonetic complement. The script loves belt
and suspenders. Chapter 07 makes a proper study of complements; for
now, just don't be surprised when a sound seems to be written twice.

So the same house sign is a word in 𓉐𓏤, a pure sound in 𓉐𓂋𓂻,
and travels with a silent classifier and a helper sound. Three jobs,
one machine:

## No vowels — on purpose

Egyptian writing records consonants only. *pr* could be pronounced
something like *per* or *par* — the script does not say, and the
true vowels are largely lost to us. This is a design choice the
script shares with its Semitic neighbors (and with modern Arabic and
Hebrew): the consonant skeleton identifies the word; the reader,
who speaks the language, supplies the rest. When Egyptologists say
hieroglyphic words aloud, they insert a conventional *e* — *per*,
*djed*, *nefer* — purely to make them pronounceable. It is a crutch,
openly worn, and this course will use it too.

## Sound-signs come in sizes

The house 𓉐 spells *two* consonants at once, p+r. Egyptian has
sound-signs for two-consonant chunks (biliterals) and even
three-consonant chunks (triliterals) — hundreds of them, and they do
much of the script's daily work. But underneath them sits a small,
closed set of signs that spell exactly **one consonant each**: the
uniliterals. There are about two dozen — a sound-kit that can, in
principle, spell anything in the language, and that every scribe
learned first. That kit is the next chapter, and from it on, every
sign this course puts in a reading will be one you own. Two you
have already met without ceremony: the speaking man's neighbors
𓆓 and 𓂧 will introduce themselves properly there.

## The same machine, twice

If you have read
[Cuneiform 101's chapter on this]({{ '/cuneiform/101/03-how-signs-mean/' | relative_url }}),
the symmetry deserves a moment of respect: word-signs, sound-signs
built by rebus, and silent classifiers — worked out on the Euphrates
in wet clay and on the Nile in carved stone, most likely
independently (whether a bare *rumor* of writing crossed between
them is debated; the machinery itself was not copied — the two
systems share no signs and no values). Two scripts that look
nothing alike are, at the level that matters, the same idea. That is
why this school teaches *writing systems* and not just scripts: the
design recurs because the problem — fitting a whole language through
a narrow channel of signs — recurs.

## Drill before you go on

1. Explain the three jobs to an imaginary student using only the
   house sign and its friends.
2. Why does a script without vowels need determinatives more than an
   alphabet does?
3. What does the small stroke 𓏤 beside a sign tell the reader?
4. English "sun" and "son" sound alike. Which Egyptian device would
   keep them apart in writing?
