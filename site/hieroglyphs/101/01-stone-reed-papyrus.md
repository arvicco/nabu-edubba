The flowering reed is among the five most frequent signs in the corpus,
and the very plant this chapter is about: the marshes that gave
Egypt its writing surface gave the script one of its busiest signs.
Draw it — one stem, one bent flower head — and read on about what
reeds did for writing.

Cuneiform is what happens when writing meets damp clay. Egyptian
writing is what happens when writing meets *two* surfaces at once —
stone that lasts forever, and papyrus that writes fast — and the
result was not one script but a pair, kept side by side for three
thousand years.

## The carved script

The hieroglyphs of this course are the *monumental* form: signs
carved in relief or incised into temple walls, tomb doorways, stelae,
and statues, and often painted. Carving rewards care, and care
preserved the pictures. An owl 𓅓 carved in 2500 BCE and an owl
carved in 300 BCE are recognizably the same bird, feather for
feather — a stability with no parallel in cuneiform, whose pictures
dissolved into abstract wedges within centuries. The difference is
the tool: a stylus *pressing* clay wants straight strokes; a chisel
*drawing* in stone will happily follow a curve, a beak, a claw.

Stone is slow, and that is the point. What went on stone was meant
to be read forever: names, titles, offerings, the deeds of kings,
the addresses of the dead. When this course reads real monuments,
you will notice how much of eternity is, in practice, *names* —
which is why chapter 05 can promise you real kings so early.

## The fast script

Nobody runs a granary with a chisel. From almost the beginning,
Egypt kept a second, everyday form of the same writing: **hieratic**,
the same signs written quickly with a rush brush and ink, on papyrus,
on wooden boards, on flakes of limestone and broken pottery
(*ostraca* — the ancient scratch paper). In hieratic the owl is no
longer visibly an owl; it is a fast, practiced squiggle that *means*
the owl-sign. Accounts, letters, receipts, school exercises, and
most literature lived in ink. Later still, an even faster
development of it, demotic, took over daily business.

This course teaches the carved forms — they are the legible
foundation, and they are what museums and monuments show you. But
keep the pair in mind: it explains how a script of careful pictures
could serve a working bureaucracy. The pictures were the Sunday
best; the ink did the errands.

## Papyrus

Papyrus is a marsh reed. Egyptians cut its pith into strips, laid
them in two crossed layers, pressed and dried them, and polished the
sheet smooth — a writing surface light enough to roll, smooth enough
for a brush, durable enough that texts on it survive after four
thousand years in Egypt's dry sand. It was one of Egypt's great
exports; the Greek word *papyros* eventually gave most of Europe its
word for paper. Clay preserved Mesopotamia's records by being
fireproof; Egypt's survived by staying dry. Every ancient archive is
an accident of climate.

## Which way do the birds look?

right-to-left — and on a symmetrical doorway, both at once, one

Signs also do not march single file. Carved text packs short signs
into tidy invisible squares — a tall sign standing alone, two or
three flat signs stacked. This site prints signs in one line, left
to right, so each sign can sit beside its transliteration; when an
arrangement matters, a figure will show it. The originals are always
cited, so the stone's own layout is never more than one link away.

## What survives

Dry sand and stone monuments have left an enormous record: temple
walls and tombs by the thousand, papyri by the tens of thousands,
ostraca beyond counting. The chapters ahead draw their readings from
a corpus of over two hundred thousand digitized passages — accounts
and hymns, tomb walls and love songs. You will start, as Egypt's own
apprentice scribes did, with the small set of signs that unlocks the
rest.
