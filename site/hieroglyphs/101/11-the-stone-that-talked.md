# The stone that talked

In July 1799, soldiers rebuilding a fort near the town of Rashid —
Rosetta — pried out a broken slab of dark stone with three bands
of writing on it. The top band was hieroglyphs, which no living
person could read and hadn't for well over a thousand years. The
bottom band was Greek, which any educated European could read at
sight. The middle was something in between. Every scholar in
Europe understood within weeks what that meant: the same text,
three times. A key, if anyone could turn it.

This chapter is the story of the turning — and you arrive at it
armed, because the two signs that resisted longest are this
chapter's batch.

Egyptian tomb owners captioned their livestock the way we caption
photographs, and herd after herd marches across the walls labeled
*young longhorn, young longhorn, young longhorn*. Remember that
homely little phrase: the sign in it is about to help crack the
script.

## The hunch about the rings

The Greek band of the Rosetta Stone is a decree honoring King
Ptolemy V. The hieroglyphic band, whatever it said, kept
repeating one group of signs inside a rope ring — and you know
from chapter 05 what a cartouche wraps: a royal name. If the rings
held "Ptolemy," then a foreign king's name — spellable only sound
by sound, as chapter 05 explained — would hand over the value of
every sign inside it. That was the hunch. Here is the payoff,
kept exactly as promised:

Walk it: 𓊪 *p* — 𓏏 *t* — 𓍯 the lasso, carrying *o* — 𓃭 the lion,
borrowed for *L* — 𓅓 *m* — 𓇋𓇋 *y* — 𓋴 *s*. *Ptolmys*: Ptolemy.
Seven sounds, one Greek king, and not a single box left. The
chapter 05 exhibit is now simply a reading.

## The cross-check: Cleopatra

A single name could be a lucky guess. Science wants a second
sample — and on an obelisk from Philae, another ring supplied one.
If the values from Ptolemy's ring were *real*, they had to work in
a different name, at different positions. Here is that ring, one
sign still beyond this course:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script"><span class="nobr">𓍹𓈎𓃭𓇋𓍯𓊪𓄿𓂧𓂋𓄿𓏏▢𓁐𓍺</span></span><span class="translit">Qljwpꜣdrꜣ,t</span><span class="gloss">Cleopatra — with chapter 09's seated woman closing the queen's name, and one ▢ (an egg, another feminine marker) for a later day</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    As carved on the Philae obelisk (the Bankes obelisk, Kingston
    Lacy) — exhibit. The signs shown are the standard spelling;
    read it against Ptolemy's ring above.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Now do what the decipherers did. The lion 𓃭 — L — is Ptolemy's
fourth sign; in Cleopatra it must come second. It does. The reed
𓇋 — third in Cleopatra, sixth (doubled) in Ptolemy. The lasso 𓍯
— O in both, third and fourth. The mat 𓊪 — P — first in Ptolemy,
fifth in Cleopatra. Sign after sign lands exactly where the Greek
spelling demands. One wobble: Cleopatra's T is written with the
hand 𓂧, which usually carries *d* — the two sounds sat close
enough for scribes to swap, and even that wobble taught the
decipherers something true about how the script breathed. Two
names, one consistent alphabet: the rings were readable, and
therefore everything was.

## Which way does a line run?

One more tool the decipherers needed, and so do you. This course
prints every reading left-to-right, and told you so in chapter 01
— but real walls run whichever way the architecture wanted, and
half the time that is right-to-left. The rule is a compass, and
the animals are the needle:

## The leap: Ramses

The rings proved the alphabet worked for *foreign* names. The
question that kept the field awake was whether the sound-signs
reached further — into Egyptian itself. In 1822 Champollion, who
had taught himself Coptic (Egyptian's great-grandchild, still
spoken in church) precisely for this moment, looked at a cartouche
copied from a temple at Abu Simbel:

<figure class="composition">
  <p class="composition-row script" aria-label="Ra, ms, s — the name Ramses">
    <span>𓇳</span><span class="op">+</span><span>𓄟</span><span class="op">+</span><span>𓋴</span><span class="op">=</span><span>𓇳𓄟𓋴</span>
  </p>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    <em>Rꜥ-ms-s</em> — Ramses. Composed here from taught signs in
    the standard core spelling; the monuments dress it with
    complements and epithets, but this is the skeleton Champollion
    saw.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

He knew 𓇳 from every temple: Ra, the sun. He knew *ms* from the
Coptic word for "born." He knew 𓋴 from the rings. *Ra-mes-es* —
"Ra bore him" — a name every classicist knew from the Greek
historians: Ramses. The sound-signs were not a special trick for
foreigners; they were the script's own machinery, and Egyptian
royal names had been sentences all along, just like Meni's
neighbors in chapter 07. Champollion ran to his brother's office,
announced *"Je tiens l'affaire!"* — I've got it — and fainted.
He was out for five days. When he woke, everything you have done
in this course had become possible.[^decipher]

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓃭 and 𓍯 from memory; say *rw*, *wꜣ*.
2. Read Ptolemy's cartouche aloud, sign by sign, without the
   gloss. You have waited six chapters to do this.
3. In Cleopatra's ring, point to the three signs that also appear
   in Ptolemy's, and say which position each takes in both.
4. State the compass rule in one sentence, and say which way this
   course prints its lines.
5. Why did Ramses's name settle the whole argument? One sentence.
