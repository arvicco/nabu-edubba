# Names in rings

You already own twenty-six sounds. That is enough, right now, to read
the name of a king who has been dead for four thousand three hundred
years. This chapter hands you three more signs and then puts them to
work immediately, on stone.

Draw all three now. 𓍷 rewards a slow hand: it is one continuous loop
with a small tie at one end, not two separate strokes.

## The ring around a name

Egyptian scribes did not leave a royal name loose on the line. They
drew a rope around it — a single loop, knotted at one end, that
marks everything inside as a king's or queen's name and nothing else.
On stone, carved in relief, it is one unbroken oval. This site prints
it as two half-brackets, 𓍹 at the start and 𓍺 at the close, so that
you can always see where the name begins and ends without losing the
line of text around it. Both conventions draw the same rope; only the
typography differs.

Three signs — 𓏏 twice, 𓇋 once — spell the name of a man who ruled
Egypt before 2300 BCE. Read it again. That is not a reconstruction.
That is his name.

## Reading 2 — cold, with a gap

Pepi came to the throne two reigns after Teti. His name spells its
final sound generously, and it ends in a sign you have not met:

Read it cold before checking the gloss: 𓊪 *p*, 𓊪 *p*, then the reed
doubled 𓇋𓇋 and the y-mark 𓏭 — three signs all spelling the name's
final glide, belt and suspenders in the manner of chapter 03.
Egyptologists render it *Ppj*: Pepi. The box at the end tells you
only that a person is meant; it
does not spell a sound. You will own it when chapter 09 opens the
classifiers of people and titles.

## The name carved first

Egyptian scribes had a courtesy rule with real teeth: when a king's
or a god's name shared a phrase with other words, the name was carved
*first*, out of respect — even when grammar reads it last. This is
honorific transposition, and it is not a scholarly abstraction; you
can see it happen.

An estate name from Teti's reign means, in full, "Ptah-satisfies-Teti"
— the god Ptah doing something *for* the king. Grammatically Teti's
name sits at the end of the phrase. On the stone it sits at the head:

Look at the sign order against the reading order: *Ttj* is carved
first, 𓏏𓏏𓇋, and read last. Nobody made a mistake. The scribe put the
king's name where the eye lands first, and trusted the reader to sort
out the grammar. You just caught a four-thousand-year-old act of
deference, in the order of the signs themselves.

## The rings that cracked the script

One more ring, as a preview, not a graded reading. Foreign rulers of
Egypt had no hieroglyphic name of their own, so their names had to be
spelled the way any foreign word is spelled — sound by sound. That
single fact turned the cartouche into the crowbar of decipherment: a
ring around a foreign name was a ring around pure phonetics, with
nothing pictorial hiding the sounds.

  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script"><span class="nobr">𓍹𓊪𓏏𓍯𓃭𓅓𓇋𓇋𓋴𓍺</span></span><span class="translit">Ptwlmys</span><span class="gloss">Ptolemy</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    As carved on the Rosetta Stone, British Museum EA 24. Shown here
    as an exhibit; read in full, with its decipherment story, in
    chapter 11.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

You can already sound out most of it: 𓊪 *p*, 𓏏 *t*, 𓅓 *m*, 𓇋𓇋 *y*,
𓋴 *s*. Two faces are new — 𓍯, a lasso, carrying the *o*-sound, and
𓃭, a lion, carrying *L*. Egyptian had no native sign for *L*; the
lion was borrowed to spell a sound the language itself did not need.
Champollion's whole breakthrough started with rings exactly like this
one.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw the cartouche — the rope loop, tie and all — from memory.
2. Write Teti's name, 𓏏𓏏𓇋, inside a ring of your own drawing.
3. Read Pepi's name cold, without looking at the gloss above.
4. Explain to an imaginary student why a lion sits in Ptolemy's name.
