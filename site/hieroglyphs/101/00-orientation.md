## The promise

You have just read three hieroglyphs — an owl, a ripple, a mouth
that are, at the same time, the sounds *m*, *n*, *r*. By the end of
this course's opening stretch you will own the small kit of signs
every Egyptian scribe learned first,
read real royal names inside their rings —

— including the two names that cracked the entire script, and finish
by reading Egypt's most-written sentence, the offering formula
𓊵𓏙𓇓, whole. Every reading is a genuine ancient text, cited to
its source.

What this course does **not** do: teach you the Egyptian language.
That is the work of the Middle Egyptian literacy track, which
assumes everything taught here. 101 is about the writing system
itself — the machine, before the language that ran on it.

## What hieroglyphs are, in three sentences

Hieroglyphic writing is the monumental script of ancient Egypt:
pictures of birds, tools, people, and water, carved in stone and
painted on walls, that record language — sounds and words, not
picture-stories. It appeared before 3000 BCE, close in time to
cuneiform, and stayed in use for three and a half thousand years,
alongside its own faster handwritten forms for everyday work. The
pictures never stopped being pictures — an owl stayed recognizably
an owl for three millennia — and that is precisely what misled
Europe for centuries into reading them as mystical symbols instead
of what they are: a working script, with sound values, spelling
habits, and school exercises.

## The funny letters — a two-minute primer

Beside every reading, this course prints the word in Latin letters
— and some of them will look like typos: *ḥ*, *ꜣ*, *ḏ*, little
hooks and dots everywhere. They are not typos. Egyptian had sounds
English does not, so Egyptologists gave each one a letter of its
own; this is the field's standard alphabet, and every book you
ever open on Egypt uses it. Two minutes here saves confusion for
the rest of the course.

Most letters mean what you expect: *b p f m n r h s k g t d w y*
sound as written. These are the strangers:

And the marks that ride along in transliterations — none of them
are sounds:

- **No vowels are written, ever.** Egyptian script recorded
  consonants only; the vowels are lost. To say a word aloud,
  Egyptologists drop in a flat "e" and read *ꜣ* and *ꜥ* as "a":
  *nfr* becomes "nefer," *ḥtp* becomes "hotep," *Ppj* becomes
  "Pepi." These are reading conveniences, not recovered
  pronunciations — <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/03-how-signs-mean/' | relative_url }}">chapter 03</a> tells the whole story.
- **≡** joins a suffix pronoun to its word: *zꜣ≡f*, "his son" —
  the *≡f* is "his."
- **Commas and dots** (*zꜣ,t*; *msi̯.t*) are editors' seams,
  separating a word's endings so you can see them; the stone
  writes no punctuation.
- **i̯** — an *i* with a little bowl under it — marks a "weak"
  final consonant of certain verbs that the spelling usually
  drops. Pronounce nothing; read past it.
- **Brackets** ([…], parentheses) mark text the editors restored
  or filled in; you met the same convention in every scholarly
  edition ever printed.

You do not need to memorize this table now. It lives here, at the
front door, so that when a *ḏ* or an *ꜣ* walks past in <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/101/04-your-first-signs/' | relative_url }}">chapter 04</a>
you can flip back one page instead of wondering whether your
screen is broken. The special terms in the table carry their own
hover-notes wherever they appear, and the
[glossary]({{ '/terms/' | relative_url }}) keeps the full list.

## The shape of the course

Chapters build strictly on one another; read them in order.

- **01–02** give you the *material* story: stone and papyrus, chisel
  and brush — and where the signs came from.
- **03** is the spine of the course: how signs carry sound and
  meaning. Read it twice.
- **04** puts the scribe's kit in your hands: the one-consonant
  signs, chosen by the same two rules as everywhere in Edubba —
  corpus frequency and graphic simplicity.
- **05** is the first summit: royal names in their rings, read from
  real monuments — the same names that deciphered the script.
- **06** reads the offering formula whole: Egypt's most-written
  sentence, assembled entirely from signs you own.
- **07–12** (later stretches) keep going: two- and three-consonant
  signs, numbers, gods and titles, the script's cursive family, the
  decipherment story in full, and the reference.

Two commitments hold everywhere in Edubba. **Nothing untaught:** a
reading will never use a sign you have not been given — a rule our
build system enforces mechanically, not aspirationally. **Real
texts:** every reading is a genuine ancient document, cited to its
source in the [Nabu](https://arvicco.github.io/nabu/) library, so
you can always follow a text back to the stone.

## How to study

- **Draw everything.** Hieroglyphs reward the pen even more than
  wedges do: each sign is a small drawing with a fixed, simple
  scheme. A sign you have drawn ten times is yours.
- **Small sessions beat marathons.** The inventory grows chapter by
  chapter; revisit old signs each session before meeting new ones.
- **Say the sounds aloud.** *m*, *n*, *r* stick to the ear faster
  than the owl, the ripple, and the mouth stick to the eye.
- **Trust the pictures.** Unlike cuneiform, where the pictures
  dissolved into wedges, a hieroglyph shows you what it is. Naming
  what you see — "viper", "basket", "bolt" — is a legitimate memory
  aid the ancients themselves used.
