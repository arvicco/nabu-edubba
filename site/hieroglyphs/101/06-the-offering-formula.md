# The offering formula

Every writing tradition has one sentence it wrote more than any
other. Mesopotamia's scribes drilled royal titularies. Egypt's
carved the **offering formula** — on tomb doorways, on stelae, on
coffins, tens of thousands of times across two and a half thousand
years. It bought the dead their bread and beer in the next world,
and it will buy you your first complete Egyptian sentence.

## The formula, assembled

The classic opening runs *ḥtp-ḏi̯-nswt* — literally "an offering
that the king gives". Watch the carved order:

<p class="script sign-strip" aria-label="The offering formula opening">𓇓&thinsp;𓏙&thinsp;𓊵&thinsp;𓊪</p>

Read the signs: sedge (*nswt*) — conical loaf (*ḏi̯*) — mat with
bread (*ḥtp*) — and a *p* completing the sound of *ḥtp*. But the
*reading order* is *ḥtp-ḏi̯-nswt*: the offering first, the king
last. You met this move in chapter 05 — **honorific transposition**.
The king outranks the sentence, so his sign is carved at the head of
the phrase regardless of where grammar puts him. On stone, protocol
beats syntax.

Why does the *king* give every private person's funeral offerings?
Because in theory all offerings flowed through him — the formula
politely routes your bread and beer through the palace and the gods
before it reaches your tomb. It is theology, etiquette, and
bookkeeping in four signs.

Take stock of what you just did. *ḥtp-ḏi̯-nswt* — read from taught
signs. The little grammar of the sentence — *n* "for", *n* "of" —
carried entirely by 𓈖, chapter 04's water ripple. The *ka* that
receives the offering, read sign by sign. The boxes hide a basket, a
land-sign, two divine spellings, and a seated woman — inventory for
later stretches, not mysteries.

## The formula as a machine

Because the formula is formulaic, it is the perfect reading drill:
thousands of surviving examples differ only in *which god*, *which
offerings*, and *whose ka*. Slot grammar, like Ur III seals in the
cuneiform school. Learn the frame once —

> *ḥtp-ḏi̯-nswt* [god's name] — [offerings] — *n kꜣ n* [the person]

— and every stela in every museum starts to run on rails you know.
The literacy track will fill the slots; the frame is now yours.

   Why don't the two orders match?

3. In the reading, find both jobs of 𓈖 — and name the chapter that
   taught you the sign.
