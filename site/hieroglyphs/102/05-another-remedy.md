# Another remedy

From the court, the temple, and the tomb, this course now walks
into the one Egyptian room the whole ancient world respected: the
doctor's. Egypt's medical papyri survive by the thousand lines —
recipes, examinations, verdicts — and their grammar is as clipped
and practical as a modern chart. Four new signs today, and not one
of them makes a sound. That silence is the lesson.

Four signs, zero sounds — a batch like this is why 101 taught you
early that silent signs carry meaning, not noise. Medicine leaned
on them harder than any other genre: force to drive illness out,
pellets to swallow, and the pustule-sign hanging over every
ailment's name.

## Grammar bite — the feminine t, and its agreeing n

One real piece of grammar hides inside the recipe formula, and it
is worth naming precisely. Egyptian nouns ending in **-t** are
feminine — *p,t* "sky," *s,t* "place," *pẖr,t* "remedy." And the
words that lean on a feminine noun *agree* with it, by wearing the
same *t*:

- **k.t** — "another (she)": the word for "other" with the
  feminine *t*, agreeing with an unspoken *remedy*.
- **n.t** — "of": chapter 01's little genitive *n*, also wearing
  the *t* — even "of" agrees in Egyptian.

So the header every recipe starts with — *k.t n.t …* — is
literally "another (remedy) of/for …," two feminine agreements
deep before the medicine is even named.

## The header: another remedy

The papyri run recipe after recipe, and each new one opens with
the same clipped formula:

- **k.t** — "another": chapter 02's mirror basket with the
  feminine *t* — the bite's first agreement.
- **n.t** — "for": the genitive, agreeing — the second.
- **dr** — "driving out": *d-r* spelled plainly, and your new
  striking man 𓀜 classifying the force. Egyptian medicine does
  not soothe an ailment; it *expels* it.
- **wḫd.pl** — the *wḫdw*: the mysterious pain-agents Egyptian
  doctors blamed for most internal suffering, written here with
  nothing but your new pustule-sign and the plural strokes. What
  exactly the *wḫdw* were, the corpus glosses with a question
  mark — and honest medicine keeps it.

Hundreds of recipes open exactly like this. Read *k.t n.t* once
and you have read the table of contents of Egyptian pharmacy.

  your new pellet 𓈒 — the pharmacy sign doing exactly its job.

## Bandage over it

And the tersest instruction of all — two signs of verb, and both
of them are today's:

- **wt** — "bandage": the corpus writes the verb with just its
  two classifiers — the sickness-sign 𓐎 and the working arm 𓂡.
  No sound-signs at all: a doctor's shorthand, meaning carried
  entirely by the silent pair, and your whole new batch working
  as one word.
- **ḥr** — "over, upon": the face-sign preposition from 101.
- **≡st** — "it (her)": the feminine suffix — agreeing, once
  again, with a feminine wound or dose upstream.

## Then you will die

Chapter 04's doctor said *bn m(w)t≡k* — "you will not die." The
same profession, in the same corpus, could also say the other
thing:

    <code>urn:nabu:aes:sawmedizin:OHMIH25V7FHTDJL5QMUKQSSJWY:IBcCIZNtPLXyrkwWvne24Eb440Q</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

- **kꜣ** — "then": a consequence particle — *if the above, then
  what follows* — with the talking man 𓀁 marking it as
  discourse.
- **m(w)t≡k** — "you die":
  [chapter 04's]({{ '/hieroglyphs/102/04-saying-no/' | relative_url }})
  death-word, cautious stroke and all, with "you" in the basket.

Egyptian medical texts are famous for exactly this honesty: the
examinations sort patients into "an ailment I will treat," "an
ailment I will contend with," and "an ailment not to be treated."
The grammar of the verdict is two signs and a suffix either way.
Medicine has never said more with less.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓀜, 𓂡, 𓈒, 𓐎 from memory — then say, for each, what
   *kind* of word it closes.
2. Explain *k.t n.t* piece by piece — both *t*'s, and what
   unspoken word they agree with.
3. Compose, in the recipe's voice: "another (remedy): (it) is
   made into water" — every sign already yours.
4. Read the two verdicts — chapter 04's kind one and this
   chapter's grim one — aloud, back to back, covering the
   glosses.
