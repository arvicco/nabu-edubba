# It is water

[Chapter 00]({{ '/hieroglyphs/102/00-orientation/' | relative_url }})
ended on a god saying *jnk nb-p,t* — "I am the lord of the sky" —
and promised that the sentence type behind it would open properly
here. It does. Middle Egyptian can build a complete sentence out of
two nouns and nothing else, and by the end of this page you will
have read one of the most argued-about examples in Egyptian
literature. Two signs first.

## Grammar bite — the nominal sentence

Egyptian's shortest sentence type puts two things side by side and
lets them be each other. No verb "to be" appears at all. The
commonest shape hangs the little word **pw** — "it is, that is" —
after the first noun:

> **X pw** — "it is X."

That is the whole machine. *Mw pw*: "it is water." The grammars
call this the **nominal sentence** — a sentence made of nouns —
and you have already met its other face: <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/102/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00's</a> *jnk
nb-p,t*, where an independent pronoun (*jnk*, "I") stands in the
first slot instead. First slot names, second slot identifies;
Egyptian trusts the bare arrangement to say "is."[^allen-nominal]

## Ptahhotep, mid-maxim

Now the real thing — and its famous context. The *Maxims of
Ptahhotep* is Egypt's classic of worldly wisdom: an aging vizier
handing his son advice, maxim by maxim, on how to live among
people. In the maxim on loving one's wife — feed her, clothe her,
gladden her heart, keep her long in your house — the old vizier
suddenly drops to two signs:

- **mw** — "water": your new sign, alone, carrying the word.
- **pw** — "she is / it is": 101's stool 𓊪 doing the little
  word's whole work — *pw* is spelled with *p* alone here, as it
  often is.

What did he mean? Translators have argued for a century — water
as what slips through your fingers, water as what nourishes the
field, water as what takes the shape of its vessel. The grammar,
unlike the metaphor, is beyond doubt: noun, *pw*, sentence. You
have just read a line scholars still fight about, and the fight
was never about the reading.

## The little n — "of"

Water again, from a different shelf entirely. The temple library
kept spells, and a spell needs a label:

  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓂋𓏤 𓈖 𓈗</span><span class="translit">rʾ n mw</span><span class="gloss">"a spell of water"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Temple library papyri (BBAW corpus), via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawtempelbib:QQUPR5AIGZCXTA4YSXKA6ILTAM:IBUBd98zcEGyikWLm52Te9NxGEw</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

- **rʾ** — "spell, utterance": 101's mouth 𓂋, with the upright
  stroke 𓏤 confirming "this is the thing itself."
- **n** — "of": the single ripple, standing between two nouns as
  the **genitive** — Egyptian's usual way of saying "X of Y" is
  simply *X n Y*, the grammars' *indirect genitive*.
- **mw** — "water," closing the phrase.

Keep the two ripple-signs apart in your head from today: one
ripple 𓈖 is the sound *n* and the little "of"; three stacked 𓈗
are the word *water* itself. The corpus writes both constantly,
often in the same line.

## Born like the sun

And the milk jug at work — at Amarna, in a hymn to the royal
family, where being royal means resembling the Sun itself:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓄟𓋴𓏏𓅱𓎡 𓏇𓇋 𓄟𓋴𓅱 𓇋𓏏𓈖𓇳</span><span class="translit">msi̯≡tw≡k mj msi̯.w Jtn</span><span class="gloss">"you are born as the Aten is born"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Amarna-period inscriptions (BBAW corpus), via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawamarna:XC6QAUTJCVHPLDGRSYE6AQR4AM:IBUBd8adyZ8nckyKkFUqMLPRlds</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

- **msi̯** — "to bear, give birth": 101's *ms* sign 𓄟 with its
  *s* complement 𓋴, exactly the pair you learned in
  [101's birth chapter]({{ '/hieroglyphs/101/08-born-of-son-of/' | relative_url }}).
- **≡tw** — "one": a suffix meaning "somebody, one" — with the
  next piece, "one bears you," Egyptian's way of saying "you are
  born."
- **≡k** — "you": the suffix pronoun on the verb, 101's basket.
- **mj** — "like": your new jug, doing its preposition job.
- **msi̯.w Jtn** — "the Aten is born": the birth verb again, and
  the sun-god of Amarna spelled *j-t-n* with the sun-disk 𓇳
  closing its own name — the same architecture as <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/102/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00's</a>
  *p,t*.

A verb wearing two suffixes and telling a whole story — that is
Middle Egyptian's engine, and you have now seen it run twice
without being told how it works. The wait is over next chapter:
the suffix conjugation, taken apart properly, in the mouth of His
Majesty himself.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓈗 and 𓏇 from memory; say *mw*, *mj*.
2. Compose, in transliteration: "it is the sky" (<a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/102/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00's</a>
   noun, this chapter's pattern) — then write it in signs.
3. Read *rʾ n mw* aloud and explain which ripple is which.
4. In the Amarna line, point to both suffixes and say what each
   does — then enjoy not yet knowing how the verb form works.
   That is next chapter's cliff.
