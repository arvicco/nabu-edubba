# Life, prosperity, health

Every time this course has written "Pharaoh, l.p.h.," those three
letters stood for something Egyptian scribes wrote so often they
compressed it to three signs. This chapter teaches the wish itself,
the Great House it followed, and — with the last batch of the
stretch — lets you read the openings of three of the most famous
stories in the language.

## Grammar bite — the wish, and the two greats

No new sentence type — the stretch taught its three, and they
hold. Two pieces of reading knowledge instead:

- **ꜥnḫ wḏꜣ snb** — "may he live, be whole, be healthy": three
  wish-verbs in a row, hung after a royal name or title like a
  bow after a signature. Scribes wrote it thousands of times and
  almost always in shorthand: 𓋹𓍑𓋴 — the ankh, your new drill,
  a single *s* — which Egyptology abbreviates "l.p.h." From today
  you read the signs, not the abbreviation.
- **The two greats.** *ꜥꜣ* 𓉻 is bigness — of buildings, doors,
  things; *wr* 𓅨 is greatness of standing — elders, chiefs,
  gods. The senses overlap and the corpora mix them, but the habit
  is worth holding: columns for size, swallows for rank.

- **ḏd.jn ḥm≡f** — chapter 02's formula, sign for sign: the
  storyteller's "then," the club, the falcon, the viper.

Father and son, two tales, one formula — and the course's oldest
reading habit, the compressed spelling honestly read, carries the
whole wish.

## The Great House

Where did the word "pharaoh" come from? Egyptian palaces were
called *pr-ꜥꜣ* — "the Great House" — and by the New Kingdom the
building's name had become the polite way to say *the king
himself*, exactly as "the White House announced" means the
president did. In the tale of Merire — a scribe so loyal he agrees
to descend to the netherworld in his ailing king's place — the
word is everywhere:

- **ḏd≡f n** — "he said to": the plain *sḏm≡f* with the little
  *n*, "to."
- **pr-ꜥꜣ** — "the Great House": 101's house-plan 𓉐 and your
  new column 𓉻 — the whole word "pharaoh" in two signs. From
  Egyptian *pr-ꜥꜣ* the word passed into Hebrew as *parʿō*, into
  Greek as *pharaō*, and into English as the only Egyptian word
  most people ever say.
- **ꜥnḫ-wḏꜣ-snb** — the wish again, riding the title exactly
  where the bite said it lives.

## The swallow, twice

The other great, at both of its jobs. First as rank, inside an Old
Kingdom name:

- **Rꜥw-wr** — the sun-god's name *Rꜥ* spelled *r* + *ꜥ*, then
  the swallow: "Ra is the great one" — a man's name that is a
  little nominal sentence, exactly the kind
  [chapter 01]({{ '/hieroglyphs/102/01-it-is-water/' | relative_url }})
  taught you to see.
- **swrj** — "drink": *s*, then the swallow carrying only its
  *sound* *wr*, an *r* confirming it, a *j* — and then two silent
  friends: chapter 01's water 𓈗 classifying the liquid, and the
  hand-to-mouth man 𓀁 classifying the act. The word comes from
  the *Report of Wenamun*, the travelogue of a temple official
  whose mission goes wrong in every port — offered a drink at a
  foreign court at the low point of his luck.

One bird, two jobs — meaning in a name, pure sound in a verb —
with the classifiers, as ever, silently keeping order. That is the
whole writing system, working.

## The stretch pauses here

Seven chapters, sixteen new signs, seventy-nine percent of the
corpus's sign-occurrences under your eye — and, more to the
point: three sentence types, the suffix conjugation, three
negations, the recipe register, and the royal wish, every one
taught from a real line and walked piece by piece. The tales this
stretch opened — Khufu's court, the Doomed Prince, Merire,
Wenamun, Neferti — keep going; a later stretch reads deeper into
them, and Sinuhe is still waiting where the classics keep their
best seat.

## Drill before the pause

1. Draw 𓉻, 𓅨, 𓍑 from memory; say *ꜥꜣ*, *wr*, *ḏꜣ*.
2. Write 𓋹𓍑𓋴 and read it in full — then say what each sign
   stands for and which word is almost entirely missing.
3. Explain to an imaginary friend where the word "pharaoh" comes
   from, with the two signs to prove it.
4. Read all three story-openings in this chapter cold, covering
   the glosses. You are reading the first lines of Egyptian
   literature's shelf of classics. That was the point of the
   stretch — and of the school.
