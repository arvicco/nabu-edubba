[Chapter 03]({{ '/hieroglyphs/102/03-to-do-to-give-to-be/' | relative_url }})
left you one sign short of a god. This chapter pays that debt — and
teaches Egyptian to refuse. Four signs, because the negation
chapter is where hearts, thrones, and death itself enter the

## Grammar bite — the adverbial sentence

One new sentence type today, the third and last of the stretch.
Beside the nominal sentence (*mw pw*) and the verbal sentence
(*ḏd≡f*), Egyptian builds sentences from a subject and a
*where*-phrase — nothing else:

> **X m Y** — "X is in Y." No verb anywhere; the preposition
> carries the "is."

The grammars call it the **adverbial sentence**, and when the
subject is a pronoun, Egyptian uses a special light form — *sw*,
"he" — to stand in the front slot.[^allen-adv]

## The prince in his place

The *Doomed Prince* is the New Kingdom's dark fairy tale: a king
without a son prays for one; the gods grant the boy, and the Seven
Hathors pronounce his fates — death by crocodile, by snake, or by
dog. The tale follows him as he flees his fate abroad, and at one
turn it sets him down with this chapter's whole grammar bite in
five signs:

- **sw** — "he": the light pronoun from the bite, spelled with
  101's *sw*-plant 𓇓 and the coil 𓏲.
- **m** — "in": the owl, carrying the whole "is."
- **s,t** — "place": your new throne 𓊨 with its *t*, and 101's
  house-plan 𓉐 classifying it as somewhere one can be.
- **≡f** — "his": the viper, closing the sentence.

Subject, where-phrase, done. Remember whose story this is, though:
in a tale about escaping your appointed place, "he is in his
place" is never a neutral sentence.

## Life is in it, death is in it

The temple library kept the language's most compressed sentences.
This one runs the adverbial pattern twice — and needs the cautious
stroke to write its second half:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓋹𓈖𓐍𓏛 𓇋𓅓 𓆑 𓅓𓏏𓏱 𓇋𓅓 𓆑</span><span class="translit">ꜥnḫ jm≡f m(w)t jm≡f</span><span class="gloss">"life is in it, death is in it"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Temple library papyri (BBAW corpus), via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawtempelbib:Q4OD2GDHL5HIXHU3MGUZ3PAMVM:IBUBd8Omin53S0z5mVI8PQ4r7tU</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

- **ꜥnḫ** — "life": the ankh you have known since 101, spelled
  full with its *n* and *ḫ*, the scroll 𓏛 marking it as an idea.
- **jm≡f** — "in it": the preposition *m* takes the shape *jm*
  before a suffix — the bite's pattern, one letter heavier.
- **m(w)t** — "death": *m*, *t* — and the word's own sign is not
  drawn. Your new 𓏱 stands where a death-sign belongs, because
  some pictures were too dangerous to draw on a working document.
  The scribe negated the *image* — this chapter in one sign.
- **jm≡f** — "in it," again: the refrain sealing the parallel.

Life in it, death in it — whatever *it* was (the papyrus does not
say on this line; the library liked its aphorisms sharp), the
sentence pattern carried Egypt's biggest antithesis in eight
signs.

## Grammar bite — three ways to say no

Now the refusals, all built on the spread arms:

- **n + verb-with-.n — "did not."** The past negation: *n*
  before the verb, and the verb wearing a little *.n* after its
  stem. Hold the frame *n … .n* as one thing: "did not."
- **nn — "there is no."** The arms plus a ripple: existence
  denied. *nn X* — "there is no X"; *nn X m Y* — "there is no X
  in Y" — the adverbial sentence, negated.
- **bn — "not," the spoken form.** In New Kingdom colloquial
  texts the arms trade their *n* for a *b*: *bn*. Same job, later
  accent — you will hear it from a doctor below.

## King Piye's verdict

In 728 BCE a Nubian king marched north and took Egypt city by
city. His victory stela — one of the great historical texts of the
Nile — records not just the battles but his judgments. Of the
defeated princes who came to submit, it explains why all but one
were kept from his palace:

  <div class="reading-lines">

  </figcaption>

- **n** — the spread arms, opening the refusal.
- **rdi̯.n** — "put" with the past *.n*: chapter 03's giving verb
  in its fuller *rdj* shape — 101's little give-arm 𓏙 inside the
  spelling — closing the *n … .n* frame: "did not put."
- **≡sn** — "they": the plural suffix, its three strokes 𓏥
  marking the many.
- **Jmn** — Amun, with the god-sign 𓊹 closing the name this
  time.
- **m jb≡sn** — "in their hearts": your new heart, exactly where
  the bite put it.

A conqueror from Nubia, judging Egypt's own princes on the state
of their hearts toward Egypt's own god. Grammar aside, it is one
of the sharpest sentences on any stela — and every piece of it is
now yours.

Three signs and a suffix: the sentence every patient in history
has wanted to hear.

## The eye meets its throne

The debt, paid. You met the offering formula — *ḥtp-ḏi̯-nswt*,
"an offering which the king gives" — in
[101's own chapter]({{ '/hieroglyphs/101/06-the-offering-formula/' | relative_url }}).
An Old Kingdom tomb runs it three times in a row, and the third
giver is the name you came for:

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓊵𓏙 𓇓𓏏 𓊵𓏏𓏙 𓇋𓈖𓊪 𓊵𓏏𓏙 𓁹𓊨</span><span class="translit">ḥtp-ḏi̯ nswt ḥtp-ḏi̯ Jnp,w ḥtp-ḏi̯ Wsjr</span><span class="gloss">"an offering the king gives; an offering Anubis gives; an offering Osiris gives"</span></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption class="citation">
    Old Kingdom tomb inscriptions (BBAW <em>Grabinschriften</em>),
    via Nabu,
    <code>urn:nabu:aes:bbawgrabinschriften:2DBWT4HXXRCHVBPGEHRQZYKZNE:IBUBdQQx4agDy0UKis1gJUznlBc</code>
    · license: attribution.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

- **ḥtp-ḏi̯ nswt** — the formula you have owned since 101: the
  offering-mat 𓊵, the little give-arm 𓏙, the king's sedge.
- **ḥtp-ḏi̯ Jnp,w** — "an offering Anubis gives": the
  jackal-god's name spelled plainly, *j-n-p*.
- **ḥtp-ḏi̯ Wsjr** — "an offering **Osiris** gives": 𓁹𓊨 — the
  eye of chapter 03 seated on the throne of chapter 04. Two signs,
  and you are reading the name of the lord of the dead — on a
  tomb wall, where he was needed most.

Why an eye and a throne spell *Wsjr* is a genuine open question —
Egyptologists have argued sound, symbol, and folk-spelling for a
century, and this course will not pretend to settle it. What is
settled is that you can now read it, anywhere in Egypt.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓂜, 𓄣, 𓊨, 𓏱 from memory — and say which two never
   make a sound.
2. Compose in transliteration, on the bite's pattern: "she is in
   her place"; then negate this chapter's patient's good news into
   the sentence nobody wants.
3. Read Piye's line cold, covering the gloss, and point to both
   halves of the *n … .n* frame.
4. Write *Wsjr* from memory. Enjoy that this is now a thing you
   can do.
