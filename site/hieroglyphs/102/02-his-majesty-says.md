# His Majesty says

Twice now a verb has walked past wearing suffixes —
[chapter 01]({{ '/hieroglyphs/102/01-it-is-water/' | relative_url }})
closed on one telling a whole story — and twice this course asked
you to wait. The wait ends here, in the best company Egyptian
literature can offer: the tales told at the court of King Khufu,
builder of the Great Pyramid, in the oldest story collection that
survives from Egypt. Three signs first, all three from the court.

## Grammar bite — the suffix conjugation

Here is the machine. A Middle Egyptian verb states its subject by
wearing it — the same suffix pronouns you have known since
[101's birth chapter]({{ '/hieroglyphs/101/08-born-of-son-of/' | relative_url }})
(*≡f* "his," *≡k* "your") attach to a *verb stem*, and what they
mean there is not possession but *doing*:

> **sḏm≡f** — "he hears." Stem first, subject as suffix, and the
> sentence has begun: verb first, subject second, everything else
> after.

That is the **verbal sentence**, and *sḏm≡f* — "he hears," the
grammars' own nickname for the whole pattern — is the workhorse
form of Middle Egyptian.[^allen-sdmf] The suffixes run through the
persons you already know: *ḏd≡j* "I say," *ḏd≡k* "you say,"
*ḏd≡f* "he says," *ḏd≡s* "she says," *ḏd≡sn* "they say" — and
<a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/102/01-it-is-water/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a> *≡tw*, "one," makes any of them impersonal. One more
piece and you can read a storyteller at work: slide **.jn** in
between stem and suffix — *ḏd.jn≡f* — and the verb becomes the
narrative past the tales run on: "then he said." Hold *.jn* as
the storyteller's "then"; its full anatomy is deeper in the
grammars than this course goes.

## At the court of Khufu

The Westcar Papyrus holds Egypt's oldest surviving story
collection: King Khufu, bored on his throne, commands each of his
sons to tell him a tale of wonder — magicians who part lake
waters, wax crocodiles that come alive, a prophecy of kings to
come. Every turn of the telling pivots on three words:

- **ḏd** — "say": the cobra 𓆓 and hand 𓂧 you have read since
  101 — the commonest verb in every tale.
- **.jn** — the storyteller's "then": 𓇋𓈖 sliding in right after
  the stem, exactly where the bite said it would.
- **ḥm** — "Majesty": your new club 𓍛 with the stroke 𓏤 ("the
  thing itself"), and then the falcon 𓅆 perched over the word —
  silent, marking whose majesty this is.
- **≡f** — "his": the viper, now doing subject-duty on a verb.
  *Said-then Majesty-his*: Egyptian's word order, verb first.

Four tales survive whole, and this formula opens royal speech in
all of them. Learn these three words and you hold the hinge of
every story an Egyptian schoolboy copied.

## The falcon on the pronoun

The falcon has one more trick, and it is the best detail on this
page. In a New Kingdom praise of the king, the god Amun speaks
*about the king himself* — and watch what happens to "you":

<figure class="reading reading--script">
  <div class="reading-lines">
    <div class="reading-line"><span class="script">𓆓𓂧 𓇋𓏠𓈖𓅆 𓂋 𓎢𓅆</span><span class="translit">ḏd Jmn r≡k</span><span class="gloss">"Amun says of you —"</span></div>

First line, piece by piece:

- **ḏd** — "says": the plain *sḏm≡f* this time, no *.jn* — a
  god speaks in the present.
- **Jmn** — Amun: *j-mn-n* with 101's game-board 𓏠, and the
  falcon 𓅆 closing the god's name.
- **r** — "of, about": the mouth-sign as the preposition *r*.
- **≡k** — "you": your new mirror-basket 𓎢 — **wearing its own
  falcon 𓅆.** The "you" is the king, the king is divine, and the
  scribe classified the *pronoun*. Even "you" bows when it points
  at a god.

And the second line is <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/102/01-it-is-water/' | relative_url }}">chapter 01's</a> nominal sentence, back
already: **mntk** — "you" as an independent pronoun, the
second-person twin of <a href="{{ '/hieroglyphs/102/00-orientation/' | relative_url }}">chapter 00's</a> *jnk* — then **zꜣ-Jmn**, "son
of Amun," 101's duckling 𓅭 beside the god's name. *You are the
son of Amun*: no verb, two nouns, a coronation in five signs.

## Drill before you go on

1. Draw 𓍛, 𓅆, 𓎢 from memory; say *ḥm*, nothing, *k* — and
   explain why the middle one gets no sound.
2. Conjugate *ḏd* through *≡j*, *≡k*, *≡f*, *≡s*, *≡sn* aloud, in
   transliteration only.
3. Write *ḏd.jn ḥm≡f* from memory, sign by sign, and name each
   piece's job as you go.
4. Find the falcon three times in this chapter's readings and say
   what each perch marks — a majesty, a god, a pronoun.
